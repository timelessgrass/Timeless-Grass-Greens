## The decision: state license first, then a federal file on top of it

A Head Start or Early Head Start grantee or delegate agency does not get to treat state child care licensing as the whole answer. Federal program standards apply on top of it, and the play yard is one of the places that shows. Before a play-yard surface goes in, plan for two files, not one: the state licensing documentation our [daycare turf article](/blog/artificial-turf-for-daycares/) walks through for Colorado, South Carolina and Florida, and a federal Head Start file that covers equipment and materials standards plus a maintenance routine.

That second file is [45 CFR 1302.47](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/1302.47), Safety practices, part of the Head Start Program Performance Standards. [45 CFR 1302.1](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/1302.1) describes those standards as the ones required to operate Head Start Preschool, Early Head Start, American Indian and Alaska Native, and Migrant or Seasonal Head Start programs. It reads the same wherever the center sits — a grantee in the Denver metro, the Grand Strand or northeast Florida answers to the identical federal text, on top of whatever its own state requires.

## What the federal rule actually requires

1302.47(b)(1) sets the standards a facility must meet, starting with 1302.47(b)(1)(i), which requires meeting applicable licensing requirements, and running through a facility kept clean, free of hazards, and — under 1302.47(b)(1)(ix) — "kept safe through an ongoing system of preventative maintenance."

The equipment and materials half of the rule, 1302.47(b)(2), is the one that reaches the play yard directly. It requires that "indoor and outdoor play equipment, cribs, cots, feeding chairs, strollers, and other equipment used in the care of enrolled children, and as applicable, other equipment and materials" meet standards set by the Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) or the American Society for Testing and Materials, International (ASTM). The same subsection repeats the preventative-maintenance requirement for equipment and materials specifically, not just the facility as a whole.

Two things that rule does not do:

- **It does not name artificial turf.** Like the CPSC's own playground safety handbook, it works by standard, not by product. A play-yard surface — turf, a rubber tile, poured-in-place material, anything else — is judged against CPSC or ASTM standards as applicable, the same way any other equipment or material in the center would be.
- **It does not replace the state licensing rule.** 1302.47(b)(1)(i) says facilities must meet licensing requirements first. The Colorado, South Carolina and Florida surfacing rules in our daycare article still apply in full. Head Start adds a second, federal layer of documentation and maintenance on top, it doesn't substitute for the first.

## What a professional install plans for on a Head Start play yard

Because the rule asks for standards met and maintenance documented, rather than a specific product, the planning work is about the paperwork as much as the ground. On a Head Start or Early Head Start play yard, that means going into the project with:

- **A surface system chosen with the state licensing rule in mind first.** Our [daycare turf article](/blog/artificial-turf-for-daycares/) covers what Colorado, South Carolina and Florida licensing expects for play-yard surfacing, including how loose fill and tested unitary surfaces are treated differently by state.
- **Documentation kept with the equipment file, not just the vendor's paperwork.** The rule asks for equipment and materials that meet CPSC or ASTM standards — keep the record where your program's file review will find it, not only where it was delivered.
- **A written maintenance routine**, covering who checks the play yard, how often, and what a check looks for, so the center can show the "ongoing system of preventative maintenance" 1302.47(b)(1)(ix) and 1302.47(b)(2)(v) require rather than describe it after the fact.
- **The design and layout planned around active supervision and any separation the program needs between infant/toddler and preschool play areas**, since 1302.47(b)(2) also asks that equipment support appropriate supervision and age separation.

## Questions to settle before the project is final

Get these answered, in writing where you can, before turf or any other surface is ordered for a Head Start play yard:

1. **What does your Head Start program specialist or grant officer expect to see on file** for equipment and materials standards under 1302.47(b)(2)? The regulation sets the requirement; how your grantee documents it is a program-level answer.
2. **What does your state child care license require** for the same play yard? Confirm the state answer first — our [daycare turf article](/blog/artificial-turf-for-daycares/) has the Colorado, South Carolina and Florida detail — since the federal rule sits on top of it, not instead of it.
3. **Who owns the maintenance routine once the play yard is built**, and where will the records live between monitoring visits?
4. **Does the layout need to separate infant/toddler play from preschool and school-age play**, and does the proposed surface and layout support that?
5. **What's specific to your building's age group mix and licensed capacity** that a general answer like this one can't cover? Confirm with your program specialist before the design is locked.

## A short checklist before you book

- Confirm the state licensing answer for your play yard first.
- Ask your Head Start program specialist what equipment and materials documentation they expect on file.
- Plan the maintenance routine as part of the project, not an afterthought.
- Keep both files — state and federal — together, and update them if the surface or layout changes.
