## The decision: fill under the house is limited, not banned

If your Grand Strand home sits on pilings in a V zone, the ground under it isn't a blank canvas for a turf layout. Federal flood rules draw a hard line at fill used to hold the building up, and a much softer one around fill used to finish the yard. Knowing which side of that line your layout falls on, before the design is final, is what keeps the project from stalling at permitting.

The short version: no fill for structural support, ever. Minor grading and minor nonstructural fill for landscaping, drainage, and support of things like parking slabs, pool decks, patios and walkways are allowed, as long as the fill doesn't block or divert the floodwater and waves that are supposed to pass freely under the house. That's the rule a turf-and-fill plan under an elevated home has to satisfy, and it comes from FEMA, not from local landscaping preference.

## What the rule actually requires, and where it comes from

The starting point is federal, not municipal. National Flood Insurance Program regulations at [44 CFR 60.3(e)(6)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/44/60.3) require every participating community to "prohibit the use of fill for structural support of buildings" in Zones V1-30, VE and V. Every coastal community on the Grand Strand that participates in the NFIP has adopted a version of this prohibition, because participation requires it.

FEMA NFIP Technical Bulletin 5 (March 2020), Section 7.5, spells out what's left once structural fill is off the table:

- **Minor grading** and **minor quantities of nonstructural fill** are allowed in Zone V, but only for landscaping, drainage under and around buildings, and support of parking slabs, pool decks, patios, walkways and similar site elements.
- That fill "should not prevent the free passage of floodwater and waves beneath elevated buildings, divert floodwater or waves such that building damage is exacerbated, or lead to damaging flood and wave conditions on a site or adjacent sites."
- Nonstructural fill "should be assumed to wash away" in a flood event. It can't be used in the building's foundation design calculations, and it can't be doing the job the pilings are doing.

That last point is worth sitting with: the bulletin treats fill under a V-zone home as something the flood is allowed to take. It's not there to be permanent. It's there to landscape and drain a yard that happens to sit under a house on stilts.

## What FEMA treats as generally acceptable

Because judging whether a specific fill plan will be "detrimental" is genuinely hard, and communities vary in how they handle it, FEMA's bulletin gives a working threshold rather than a case-by-case guess for every lot:

- **Up to 2 feet of fill** under or around an elevated building "can generally be assumed to comply" with the free-of-obstruction requirement, without needing an engineering analysis or certification — provided basic site drainage principles aren't violated and there's nothing site-specific that would make that quantity of fill damaging.
- **Fill type matters.** The bulletin recommends fill similar to the area's natural soils — typically clean sand or sandy soil, free of large rocks, clay, silt and organic material — so it behaves like the surrounding ground rather than as a rigid, water-blocking mass.
- **Site drainage slope matters more than depth.** A slope around 1 unit vertical to 5 units horizontal (about 20%) or shallower is described as unlikely to cause or worsen wave runup regardless of fill height. Steeper grades, especially near 1 to 3, can produce appreciable wave runup that puts the house or a neighboring structure at risk.
- **Vertical clearance under the lowest floor** should still be maintained between the top of the fill and the lowest horizontal structural member, so base flood flow and waves have somewhere to pass.

The 2-foot figure is a national baseline FEMA itself calls a starting assumption, not a cap every jurisdiction has to honor. A community can set a lower threshold, or require an engineering analysis at a shallower depth than 2 feet if local conditions warrant it. That's a question for your county floodplain administrator, and it's covered in more detail in our guide to [permit steps in Brunswick and Horry County](/guides/brunswick-and-horry-county-turf-permit-steps/).

## What a professional install plans for on a V-zone lot

Turf and fill under a piling-elevated home is a site-prep problem before it's a design one. What we plan for on the free visit:

- **The flood zone designation on your specific parcel**, since V-zone rules and the adjoining coastal construction setback line are separate questions — our [oceanfront setback guide](/guides/oceanfront-turf-cccl-and-beachfront-setback/) covers the beachfront line, which sits alongside, not instead of, the flood-zone fill rule.
- **How much grade change the layout actually needs**, checked against the community's own fill-depth threshold before any base material is ordered.
- **A base built to shed water on a gentle grade**, consistent with the site-drainage slope FEMA describes, rather than a steep mound that concentrates runoff or wave runup against the pilings.
- **Fill compatible with the surrounding soil**, so the material behaves the way FEMA's guidance assumes it will in a storm.
- **Clearance under the lowest floor**, so nothing built into the yard crowds the space floodwater and waves are supposed to use.

That's the same site-prep and base-building work behind every [residential turf](/services/residential-turf/) install we do on the Grand Strand — on a V-zone lot, it's shaped around this rule specifically.

## Questions to settle before the project is final

1. **What's your parcel's flood zone and Base Flood Elevation?** That comes from your FIRM panel and, for many post-storm rebuilds, an elevation certificate you may already have on file.
2. **What fill-depth threshold does your county apply before requiring an engineering analysis?** FEMA's 2 feet is a national assumption; the local number governs.
3. **Does the layout include any drainage improvements, parking slab support, or a patio tie-in** that changes how much fill the plan actually calls for?
4. **Has an engineer already signed off on grading for the house itself,** and does that documentation say anything about landscaping fill separately?
5. **Is any part of the yard also inside a beachfront setback**, where a second, unrelated rule applies?
