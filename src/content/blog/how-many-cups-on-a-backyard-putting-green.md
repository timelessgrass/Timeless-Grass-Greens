  question: How many cups should a backyard putting green have?

## Start from the putts you want, not a number

On a golf course, the hole moves. A [USGA Green Section Record article](https://gsrpdf.lib.msu.edu/?file=%2F2000s%2F2008%2F080721.pdf) notes that a green typically needs at least 14 days for an old hole location to recover from play, so the cup keeps being cut somewhere new.

A synthetic green has nothing to recover, and its cups stay where they are set. Whatever variety it has, you build in on day one: how many cups, where they sit, and where you stand to putt at them.

So instead of picking a number, list the putts you want to hit:

- a short, straight putt on level ground, to groove the stroke;
- a short putt that breaks, to practice reading slope;
- a mid-length putt that tests speed;
- a long lag putt, if the green has the length.

Then work out the fewest cups that give you all of them. One cup can give you several putts if you change where you stand: uphill from one side, downhill from another, across the slope from a third. A second cup earns its place when it offers a putt the first can't. Two cups that both give you the same flat six-footer just use up green.

## The hole: 4¼ inches across, at least 4 inches deep

The [Rules of Golf](https://assets.randa.org/c42c7bf4-dca7-00ea-4f2e-373223f80f76/48712d47-76dc-4fd3-add1-53972c021580/2023%20Rules%20of%20Golf.pdf) define the hole the same way everywhere: 4¼ inches (108 mm) in diameter and at least 4 inches (101.6 mm) deep. A liner can be no wider than 4¼ inches and must sit at least 1 inch below the putting surface, unless the soil requires it closer. Our post on [putting and chipping greens](/blog/putting-green-or-chipping-green/) shows why it stays the same whatever the layout.

A backyard install may set the cup differently. One supplier's [installation guide](https://turfandivy.com/installation/installation-guide/putting-green-installation/) puts the rim a quarter inch below the surface and says it should never stand above the tips of the turf fibers. The same guide sizes holes for 6-inch cups as well as 4¼-inch ones. A bigger cup drops more putts, but only the regulation size matches a course.

## Give each cup its own level ring

The Green Section article describes the ground a hole needs: an area two to three feet in radius around it, kept close to level and at one even grade. Its checklist asks for at least 3 feet of consistent slope around the hole, and at least 6 feet where the hole sits on steeper ground.

Those rings set the spacing. Two cups with 3-foot rings need centers at least 6 feet apart to keep the rings from overlapping. On a slope, with 6-foot rings, that becomes 12 feet. Leave more than the minimum. The article sets holes where a putt from anywhere on the green can be stopped within about two feet, and a miss that size shouldn't end up in the next cup's ring.

Cup count and green size are one decision. Our post on [how big a backyard putting green should be](/blog/how-big-should-a-backyard-putting-green-be/) works out the footprint from those rings.

## Keep the putts from crossing

Cups set in a straight row from where you stand get in each other's way. A putt to the far cup has to roll past, or over, the near one. It can drop into the wrong cup or clip the edge and change line.

Stagger them instead, and test the layout on paper:

1. **Mark the two or three spots where you'll putt from most,** such as the edge of the patio or the path from the back door.
2. **Draw a straight line from each spot to each cup.**
3. **Move any cup whose line runs through another cup's ring.**

Cups set in a loose triangle, each approached from a different side, tend to pass that test. A breaking putt curves off that line, so leave extra room on its high side.

## Distances worth building in

A [Columbia Business School analysis](https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/pubfiles/4996/assessing_golfer_performance.full.pdf) set PGA Tour shot data from 2003 to 2010 beside amateur results. Tour players one-putt half the time from 8 feet. Golfers who average 90 for 18 holes one-putt half the time from 5 feet, and average two putts from 19 feet, against 33 feet for the pros. Those amateurs three-putt about 2.3 times a round, four times as often as tour players.

For most golfers that points to two putts worth a cup:

- **Short putts around 5 feet,** which a 90 shooter makes about half the time. A cup with a level approach from 3 to 8 feet away covers that range.
- **Lag putts of 20 feet or more,** past the point where that golfer averages more than two putts. On a rectangular green the diagonal runs farther than either side, so a cup near a far corner is how a smaller green gets that length.

Then mix them up. In a 2024 study in the [European Journal of Sport Science](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11235769/), 40 novice golfers practiced on an artificial green about 13 by 23 feet, putting from roughly 4, 8 and 12 feet. The group that switched distances in random order putted closer to the target in a test three days later than the group that practiced one distance per day. It's a small study, and its authors caution against generalizing from it. Still, several cups make that kind of practice easy: putt each ball to a different cup.

## Where each cup sits on the slope

Our post on [how fast a putting green should roll](/blog/how-fast-should-a-putting-green-roll/) covers how much slope a cup can sit on at a given speed. The Green Section checklist adds placement rules that scale down to a backyard:

- **Stay back from steep ground.** Holes go no closer than three paces to slopes so steep a ball won't stop on them.
- **Leave room to putt boldly.** A golfer above the hole should be able to putt with some confidence, not just try to keep the ball from running away.
- **Keep in from the edge.** On a course, holes sit four to five paces in, or at least 10 feet in where no hazards or steep slopes are close by. The article notes the five-pace rule doesn't suit small greens, which is why it offers the 10-foot one. At home the aim is the same: a missed putt should stay on the putting turf.

The checklist also asks for a mix of easy, moderate and harder hole locations. At home, that means at least one cup with a level, straight approach, so a session isn't all break. One line from the article fits any layout: "If you have to look long, it's wrong!"

## Flags

The Rules of Golf define the flagstick as a movable pole placed in the hole to show players where it is. At home it marks the cup from across the yard and gives you a target on long putts.

You can leave it in. Rule 13.2a lets you putt with the flagstick in the hole, with no penalty if the ball hits it.

For flag-in putts that behave like a course's, match the pole. The R&A's [Rules of Equipment](https://www.randa.org/roe/the-rules-of-equipment/part-8-committee-equipment-requirements) require a pole that is round in cross-section, no more than three-quarters of an inch (19 mm) across from 3 inches above to 3 inches below the surface, and free of anything that absorbs the ball's impact. A thicker or padded pole changes how a ball reacts when it hits the stick.

## Drainage and anchoring

A cup is a 4-inch hole in the ground, so rain collects in it unless the water has somewhere to go. The supplier guide above says that where the soil doesn't drain well, the hole should be dug 2 to 4 inches deeper than the cup with gravel added. It also says the cup must stay reachable without lifting the whole green.

Cups also move when the ground around them does. Another supplier's [installation notes](https://syntheticgrasswarehouse.com/company/ask-an-expert/ask-jw/installation-of-nylon-poly-putt/) say cups set in compacted dirt tend to shift from rainwater, foot traffic and frequent pulling of the flag, which lets the cup settle and the flag tilt. Both suppliers recommend setting cups in concrete, the first guide especially in a freeze-thaw climate. ## Cup checklist before the layout is drawn

1. **List the putts you want,** then the fewest cups that give you all of them.
2. **Mark where you'll stand** most often.
3. **Ring each cup** with 3 feet of near-level turf, 6 feet on a slope, and keep centers at least 6 feet apart (12 on a slope).
4. **Draw a line from each spot to each cup,** and move any cup whose line crosses another cup's ring.
5. **Build in a putt around 5 feet** and, if there's room, a lag of 20 feet or more.
6. **Keep cups off steep ground and in from the edge,** with at least one level, straight approach.
7. **Settle cup size, flag, drainage and anchoring** before the base goes in.
