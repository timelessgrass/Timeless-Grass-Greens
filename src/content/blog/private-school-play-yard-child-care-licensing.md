## The design question comes second to the classification question

Before a private or parochial school's business office designs a play yard, one question decides which surfacing rules apply: is this program, legally, child care? The answer isn't the same for every classroom on the same campus, and it isn't the same in every state we build in. A kindergarten room attached to an elementary school is usually treated differently from a 3-year-old preschool room down the same hallway, and a program that adds before-care or after-care hours can end up reclassified on its own. Settle the classification with the school's licensing authority first. The surfacing standard, and who has to sign off on the play yard, follows from that answer.

## Florida: the age line runs through the middle of "nonpublic school"

[Fla. Stat. 402.3025](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2025/402.3025) sets separate rules for public and nonpublic schools, and a private school's business office needs subsection (2), not (1).

Under 402.3025(2):

- **Under 3.** Programs for children under 3 years of age "shall be deemed to be child care" outright — no exemption.
- **Kindergarten and up.** Programs in 5-year-old kindergarten and grade one or above are not child care.
- **Age 3 to under 5.** These programs are not child care only if three conditions all hold: the program is "operated and staffed directly by the schools," "a majority of the children enrolled in the schools are 5 years of age or older," and the school complies with the personnel screening requirements in s. 402.305. Miss any one of those and the program falls under the child care statutes.
- **Even when exempt, standards still apply.** Under 402.3025(2)(d)1, an unlicensed 3-to-under-5 program still has to "substantially comply with the minimum child care standards" the state has adopted. Exempt from licensing isn't the same as exempt from every standard.

A school can also choose to designate a program as child care voluntarily, which brings it fully under the statute.

## South Carolina: the exclusion has an hours limit built in

[S.C. Code 63-13-20(4)](https://www.scstatehouse.gov/code/t63c013.php) defines "childcare facility" broadly, then excludes several categories, including:

- An educational facility, private or public, "which operates solely for educational purposes in grade one or above."
- Five-year-old kindergarten programs.
- "Kindergartens or nursery schools or other daytime programs, with or without stated educational purposes, operating no more than four hours a day and receiving children younger than lawful school age."

That last exclusion is the one to read carefully. It covers preschool-age programs, but only up to four hours a day. A morning preschool session that runs three hours clears it. The same program with an added afternoon block, or a wraparound care option, may not. There's no separate carve-out in this section for a program simply because a school runs it — the four-hour line is what does the work.

## Colorado: the preschool answer runs opposite Florida's

Colorado's early childhood statutes were recodified into Title 26.5 in recent years; a lookup that lands on the old citation, C.R.S. 26-6-103, is reading a repealed section. The current definition is [C.R.S. 26.5-5-303(3)(a)](https://codes.findlaw.com/co/title-26-5-early-childhood-programs-and-services/co-rev-st-sect-26-5-5-303/), and it's worth reading in full because it cuts the opposite way from Florida and South Carolina on preschool.

The "child care center" definition:

- Excludes "any kindergarten maintained in connection with a public, private, or parochial elementary school system of at least six grades" — the same kindergarten carve-out the other states make.
- But then specifically includes "those facilities for children under six years of age with stated educational purposes operated in conjunction with a public, private, or parochial college or a private or parochial school."

In plain terms: Colorado's statute names a private school's own preschool program as a child care center in the definition itself, rather than exempting it the way Florida's 3-to-under-5 carve-out does. There's no operate-and-staff or majority-age test in this section that pulls a school-run preschool back out. A Colorado private school's business office should not assume Florida's or South Carolina's exemption logic applies here; confirm the classification with the state's early childhood licensing office before assuming the preschool room is exempt.

## North Carolina: an accreditation and funding test, not just hours

[N.C. Gen. Stat. 110-86](https://web.archive.org/web/2026/https://www.ncleg.gov/enactedlegislation/statutes/html/bysection/chapter_110/gs_110-86.html), read via an archived copy after the live page refused automated access, excludes public schools from the "child care" definition outright. For nonpublic schools, the exclusion in subsection (2)f is narrower and depends on two things at once: accreditation and funding.

To qualify, the nonpublic school must be one "described in Part 2 of Article 39 of Chapter 115C" and "accredited by national or regional accrediting agencies with early childhood standards." If both hold, the school's early childhood program is excluded from the child care definition if it either:

- Operates a child care facility for less than six and a half hours a day, on or off the school site, or
- Operates one for more than six and a half hours a day, but doesn't receive NC Pre-K or child care subsidy funding.

A program that clears the accreditation bar but adds long-day wraparound care funded through NC Pre-K or a child care subsidy would fall back under the statute. That's a narrower needle than Florida's or South Carolina's.

## What a professional install plans for either way

Once the classification is settled, the play yard design follows it. A licensed program answers to the same equipment, use-zone and surfacing questions a stand-alone daycare does — we cover the surfacing side of that, including ASTM F1292 impact testing for turf and pad together, in [our article on planning turf for a daycare play yard](/blog/artificial-turf-for-daycares/). An exempt program in Florida still has to substantially comply with the state's minimum child care standards, so the same questions belong on the table even without a license number attached.

A professional install plans around whichever answer comes back:

- **Equipment fall zones.** Where climbing or play equipment sits, the ground cover under and around it needs a documented impact rating suited to the fall height, not just a general-purpose lawn product.
- **Shade.** Full-sun turf runs hot; our article on [whether artificial turf is safe for a children's play area](/blog/is-artificial-turf-safe-for-kids/) covers surface temperature and why shade placement matters over the busiest zones.
- **Drainage and base.** A play yard used daily needs a base built for consistent footing and drainage, not just appearance.
- **Cleaning and inspection routine.** Whoever runs the program needs a written routine for debris removal and surface checks, matched to how the program actually uses the space.

## Questions to settle before the project is final

1. **What is each program's classification** — infant, toddler, 3-to-under-5, kindergarten, grade one and up — under the current statute in this state, not an older or recodified citation?
2. **Does the program meet every condition for an exemption**, where one exists: who staffs it, the age mix of enrolled children, personnel screening, hours per day, accreditation, or funding source?
3. **Does adding before-care, after-care or extended hours change the classification** for any age group using the play yard?
4. **If the program is exempt, which minimum standards still apply**, and who confirms that in writing?
5. **Has the school's licensing contact confirmed the answer in writing** before the play yard's equipment layout and surfacing are finalized?
