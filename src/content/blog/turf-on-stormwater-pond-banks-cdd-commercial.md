## Start with the pond's own permit, not a general rule

A CDD supervisor, HOA board or commercial property manager looking at bare or eroding banks around a stormwater pond usually wants one answer: can turf go there. There isn't a single statewide yes or no for a permitted wet detention pond the way there is for a single-family swale. The pond was built and approved under its own environmental resource permit (ERP), and that permit is what decides what its banks have to do.

Within the St. Johns River Water Management District, most wet detention ponds serving a CDD, commercial site or planned community were designed to the standards in the [ERP Applicant's Handbook, Volume II](https://flrules.org/gateway/readRefFile.asp?refId=9405&filename=010+4b3--AH+II+SJWMD.pdf), effective June 1, 2018. Those standards set out what the pond's littoral zone and side slopes have to do, and that's the starting point before turf gets priced for any part of the bank.

## What the littoral zone requires

Section 8.6 of the handbook defines the littoral zone as the portion of a wet detention pond designed to contain rooted aquatic plants, usually the gently sloped shelf that extends down to two to three feet below the pond's normal water level. Its design criteria, in the handbook's own words:

- **Slope.** "The littoral zone shall be gently sloped (6H:1V or flatter)."
- **Coverage.** "At least 30 percent of the wet detention pond surface area shall consist of a littoral zone," measured as the ratio of vegetated littoral zone to the pond's surface area at the control elevation.
- **Plant establishment.** "Within 24 months of completion of the system or as specified by permit condition, 80 percent coverage of the littoral zone by suitable aquatic plants is required."

The handbook also allows an applicant to substitute an enlarged permanent pool volume, or added pre-treatment, in place of the vegetated littoral zone (Section 8.7). Which path a given pond took is on file in its permit, and it changes what its banks are required to do.

## What the side slopes require

Two separate provisions bear on how steep and how stabilized a pond's banks are, and they don't say the same thing:

- **Public safety (Section 2.6.1).** Normally dry basins that hold more than two feet of water, and permanently wet basins, must have side slopes "no steeper than 4H:1V out to a depth of two feet below the control elevation," unless the basin is fenced or its access is otherwise restricted.
- **Wet detention pond design (Section 8.12).** For a wet detention pond specifically, "the average pond side slope measured between the control elevation and two feet below the control elevation" must be "no steeper than 3:1 (horizontal:vertical)."
- **Stabilization (Section 2.6.2).** "All stormwater basin side slopes shall be stabilized by either vegetation or other material to minimize erosion of the basin," and the handbook directs that "the retardation of overland runoff and soil stabilization using naturally occurring vegetation coverage shall be considered before paving, riprap, lining, energy dissipation and other structural measures are employed."

That last line is the one that matters most for a turf question. The handbook's own order of preference puts vegetation ahead of a structural surface, and doesn't name synthetic turf as either category. Whether turf on a given bank satisfies the stabilization requirement, or has to be treated as a structural measure requiring its own review, is a question the pond's permit and the district's engineering staff answer, not a general rule.

## The 2024 statewide rule doesn't replace this

Florida's revised statewide stormwater rules took effect on ratification, June 28, 2024. [DEP's own FAQ](https://floridadep.gov/water/engineering-hydrology-geology/content/frequently-asked-erp-stormwater-questions) on that rule confirms design and performance standards specific to each water management district still apply alongside it. DEP also notes that applications for individual and general ERP permits deemed complete after December 28, 2025 must meet the revised statewide requirements going forward.

Neither point changes what an existing, already-permitted pond has to do. Its Volume II design criteria, set when it was permitted, stay in force unless the pond itself goes back through a modification. That's a different question from the swale standard that governs turf on a single-family lot, which our article on [turf in a retention area or swale](/blog/artificial-turf-in-a-retention-area-or-swale/) covers separately.

## What a professional install plans around

Before turf goes anywhere near a permitted pond bank, a professional plan starts with the permit file, not the ground. That means confirming, in writing:

- Whether the specific bank in question was designed as littoral zone, upland side slope, or both.
- What slope the permit shows for that stretch of bank, and how close the proposed turf area comes to the control elevation.
- What stabilization method the permit lists, and whether the district treats a synthetic surface as meeting it.
- Whether any part of the littoral zone's required plant coverage would be reduced by the project.

## Questions to settle before the project is final

1. **Pull the pond's own ERP file.** The district or your engineer of record can confirm whether the bank in question is littoral zone, side slope, or a buffer with its own rule.
2. **Get the slope and stabilization requirement in writing** for that specific bank, not a general standard for the pond type.
3. **Ask whether a permit modification is needed** before any structural or synthetic surface goes on a stabilized slope or littoral shelf.
4. **Confirm who signs off in your community** — for lot-level turf that's usually the association, but the pond itself is district infrastructure. Our article on [who approves turf in a CDD community](/blog/cdd-or-hoa-who-approves-turf/) covers that split.
