## Start with the permit, not the surface

In Florida, dogs on a restaurant patio start with a local decision. [Section 509.233](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2026/509.233) lets a city or county create an exemption from parts of the federal Food Code, as the state has adopted it, so patrons' dogs can sit in designated outdoor areas.

Nothing makes a local government do this. Adoption is "at the sole discretion" of each governing body. So your first call is to your city, or your county if you're outside city limits. Ask whether it has an ordinance, and for the application.

If it does, you need a permit before any dogs arrive. The application has to include at least:

- **Your restaurant's name, location and mailing address,** plus the applicant's contact details.
- **A diagram of the dog area.** It shows the dimensions, the tables, chairs and equipment, the entrances and exits, the boundaries, any fences or barriers, and nearby sidewalks and property lines. It must be accurate and to scale, but the statute doesn't require a design professional to draw it.
- **The days and hours** dogs will be allowed.

The permit doesn't pass to a buyer. If you sell the restaurant, it expires and the buyer has to apply again.

## What the law asks of the ground

Every local ordinance has to include a set of minimum rules. Several of them decide what the ground under your tables has to handle:

- **Accidents.** Dog waste accidents "shall be cleaned immediately" and the area sanitized with an approved product. A kit for the job has to be kept near the dog area.
- **Spills.** Spilled food and drink must be removed from the floor or ground between seatings.
- **Tables and chairs.** Their surfaces are cleaned and sanitized with an approved product between seatings. Dogs can't sit on them.
- **Leashes.** Dogs stay on a leash and under reasonable control.
- **Access.** Dogs can't pass through indoor areas, or through outdoor areas that aren't designated for dogs. Getting in and out can't require going through the building.

The statute doesn't name any surface material. It doesn't say which products count as approved, either. Ask the office that issues your permit. The state agency involved is the [Division of Hotels and Restaurants](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2025/509.013), part of the Department of Business and Professional Regulation. Your city or county sends it copies of permits and reports complaints to it. Local rules can go further than the state minimum, so get the ordinance itself, not a summary.

## Why turf makes sanitizing harder to get right

Paving is one flat face. Turf is fibers standing on a backing, usually with infill between them. That matters for the second half of the cleaning duty.

Iowa State's [guide to disinfection in animal settings](https://www.cfsph.iastate.edu/Disinfection/Assets/Disinfection101.pdf) was written for farms, shelters and clinics, not restaurants. Its principles still apply to a patio where dogs have accidents:

1. **Clean before you disinfect.** Dirt, feces and body fluids "can hide organisms" and can make several disinfectants stop working.
2. **Mind the gaps.** Pits, pores and other surface irregularities "can serve as reservoirs for pathogens."
3. **Keep it wet long enough.** The surface has to stay wet with disinfectant for the full contact time. The guide notes that can be a challenge in high temperatures.

The [EPA's disinfectant checklist](https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2020-04/documents/disinfectants-onepager.pdf) adds a step that settles a lot: read the label's "use sites" and "surface types." If the product your permit office accepts isn't labeled for the surface you chose, you can't sanitize that surface by the label.

The turf industry's own advice points the same way. The Synthetic Turf Council's maintenance guidelines were written for sports fields and are voluntary. They say to:

- Remove organic material, including animal waste, as soon as possible.
- Keep food, drinks and chewing gum off the turf.
- Avoid cleaning chemicals with alcohol or acetone solvents, and ask before using any chemical.
- Keep high-pressure sprays under 500 psi, since stronger sprays may damage the turf.

A dining patio brings food, drinks and dogs together every service. Dropped food lands in the fibers. Accidents happen mid-shift. Staff need a fix that works in minutes.

## A layout that splits the jobs

You don't have to pick one surface for the whole area. Put each surface where its cleaning job fits.

- **Under tables and along the serving path, use paving.** Crumbs and spilled drinks land here. Staff can scrape, wash, sanitize and keep the surface wet for the contact time.
- **Use turf where it adds the most and gets the least spilled food.** That might be a border, a waiting spot by the entrance, or a strip away from the tables. If it's inside the dog area, draw it on the permit diagram.
- **Give the dogs their own entrance.** It has to reach the patio without passing through the building.
- **Plan where rinse water goes.** Sanitizer and wash water run somewhere. Keep that path off seating areas and away from the building.

If the whole dog area has to be turf, ask the permit office before you commit. Bring the product sheet and the turf maker's care instructions, and ask whether its accepted sanitizer suits that surface.

## Before you choose a turf

Ask the turf maker, in writing, which cleaners and disinfectants the fibers, backing and infill tolerate. Match that list against the label of the product your permit office accepts. If the two don't overlap, choose paving for that spot.

Ask about infill too. Infill sits between the fibers, so it's where dropped food and waste can settle. Ask whether the turf needs infill at all, what the infill is made of, and how staff can clean it.

## Patio checklist

- Confirm your city or county has adopted a dog-friendly dining ordinance.
- Get the ordinance text and the permit application.
- Ask the permit office which sanitizing products it accepts.
- Check each product's label for surface types and contact time.
- Get the turf maker's cleaner list in writing.
- Draw the dog area to scale, with tables, exits, barriers and the turf edge.
- Plan a dog entrance that doesn't go through the building.
- Keep the cleanup kit next to the dog area, and show every server where it is.
- Mark where rinse water drains before anything is installed.
