## Start with what you actually want to practice

A putting green and a chipping green solve different problems, and the difference shows up underfoot before it shows up in the score.

A putting-only layout is one surface, built so a ball rolls true and at a consistent speed from any starting point. Every shot on it begins on the green.

A chipping, or chip-and-putt, layout adds a landing area short of the putting surface. A pitched or chipped ball comes in from off the green, needs somewhere to land, check and settle, and only then reaches the putting turf itself. That landing area is what changes the footprint, the turf and the questions worth asking before a layout is drawn.

Neither is the "right" choice on its own. It comes down to what shots you want at home, and how much yard you have to give it.

## What changes underfoot

The putting surface itself is built for one job: a true, consistent roll. The turf, the base beneath it and the contours are all set up around that.

A landing area for chips and pitches asks something different of the turf. It has to let a ball come in from the air, land, and check up rather than skid straight through onto the green. That usually means a separate turf zone with a taller, softer pile than the putting surface, sitting between the rough and the green.

One manufacturer's own golf turf line describes this zone, which it calls fringe or collar turf, as ["slightly higher than the putting green, providing a seamless transition from the rough to the green and a realistic play experience."](https://www.synlawngolf.com/golf-turf/fringe/) That height difference is the underfoot change: a shorter, denser surface where the ball rolls, and a taller transition surface around it where the ball lands.

A pure putting green may still carry a short collar for edge definition, but it doesn't need a landing zone sized for an incoming shot. A chip-and-putt layout does.

## The hole doesn't change

Whichever layout you choose, the cup itself is built to the same specification. Under the current [Rules of Golf](https://assets.randa.org/c42c7bf4-dca7-00ea-4f2e-373223f80f76/48712d47-76dc-4fd3-add1-53972c021580/2023%20Rules%20of%20Golf.pdf), effective since 1 January 2023, the hole is defined as "the finishing point on the putting green for the hole being played," and:

- It must be 4¼ inches (108 mm) in diameter and at least 4 inches (101.6 mm) deep.
- If a lining is used, its outer diameter can't exceed 4¼ inches, and it must sit at least 1 inch below the putting surface, unless the ground itself calls for it closer to the surface.

That's a fixed target regardless of what surrounds it. A putting-only green and a chip-and-putt layout both play to the same cup. What changes around it is the surface a ball has to cross to get there.

## How much room a chipping area actually takes

A putting green can be shaped to fit a tight, oddly cut yard, since the whole surface is in play from the first stroke. A landing area changes that math.

To be useful, the landing turf needs enough distance from wherever you'll be pitching or chipping from to let a ball come down, check, and settle before it reaches the putting surface. How much distance that takes depends on the shots you actually want to hit and the shape of the yard around them, not a fixed number that applies to every property. That's a layout decision, not a guess, which is why it gets settled on-site rather than from a sketch.

Some yards have the room for both a chipping area and a full putting surface. Others fit a putting green comfortably but would have to shrink it, or cut into a fairway strip or side yard, to add a landing zone. Either can work. The question is which shots matter enough to you to give up the yard space for them.

## Questions to settle before the layout is final

1. **What shots do you actually want to practice?** Putting only, or pitches and chips coming in from off the green?
2. **How much yard can the layout use?** A landing area needs room the putting surface alone doesn't.
3. **Where does the transition zone sit, and how wide?** That's a site-specific call, made on the ground.
4. **Does your community require plan approval?** Some do; know that before design starts.
5. **What's the cup count and placement?** More than one hole location changes how much putting surface you need.
