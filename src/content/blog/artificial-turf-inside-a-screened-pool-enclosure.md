## What the cage changes, and what it doesn't

A screened lanai or pool cage changes the debris that lands on the turf and, often, how the pool barrier is built. It doesn't change the drainage math underneath the turf, and it doesn't seal the space off from rain.

Plan a screened-in lawn or pet run the way you'd plan an open one — same base, same slope, same runoff destination — then layer in the two things that are specific to a cage: what the screen door has to do for pool safety, and whether the lot backs onto water the county protects.

## Rain still gets in

Most residential pool cages are mesh from the footers to the roofline, not a solid roof. Rain falls through the same as it does outside the cage; the screen's job is keeping leaves, pollen and most insects out, not keeping water out.

That matters because Florida's statewide synthetic turf standard, [Rule 62-308.100, F.A.C.](https://flrules.org/gateway/readFile.asp?sid=0&tid=30839130&type=1&file=62-308.100.doc), effective 19 May 2026, applies to single-family properties of 1 acre or less and doesn't distinguish covered ground from open ground. It requires:

- **Permeable turf on permeable backing,** over a subgrade prepared for positive drainage.
- **No pooling, and no added runoff** onto a neighboring property; water should reach pervious ground on your own lot where possible.
- **Natural infill,** installed so it doesn't wash off the property.

Inside a cage, that last point works a little differently. Sand that would normally scatter across an open lawn instead has nowhere to go but the enclosure's own hardscape — the pool deck, a paver walk, the footers the screen frame sits on. A firm, compacted edge where turf meets deck keeps infill out of the pool and off the pavers, the same job it does at an open pool's coping.

## The screen door can be the pool barrier

Florida law doesn't require a fence specifically. [Fla. Stat. 515.27](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2025/515.27) lets a residential pool pass inspection with any one of five features: a barrier, an approved safety cover, exit alarms on the doors and windows that reach the pool, a self-closing and self-latching door, or a pool alarm. A screen door that closes and latches on its own can be that feature.

If that's how your pool passed inspection, the layout has one job the open-yard version doesn't: leave the door working. Before the turf goes in, note:

- **Which feature your pool actually uses.** If it's the self-latching door, that door's swing and threshold matter more than anything else in the plan.
- **The finished height at the threshold.** [Fla. Stat. 515.29](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2025/515.29) requires a barrier — where one is in use — to measure at least 4 feet high on the outside, and to leave nothing a young child could use to climb it. A raised turf edge or an uneven seam at a screen door track can undercut either point.
- **The latch itself.** Once the turf is down, open the door and let it go. It should swing shut and catch without dragging.

Where the enclosure instead sits behind a separate fence — common when the cage covers the whole yard and the actual pool barrier is further out — the same fence rules from our [open-pool-deck article](/blog/artificial-turf-around-a-pool/) still apply; the screen is a second layer, not a replacement.

## If the yard backs onto a pond

A lot of Northeast Florida's screened lanais back straight onto a stormwater pond or a wetland edge — it's common across Nocatee, Oakleaf Plantation, Fleming Island and similar communities built around a lake system. That changes what the turf plan has to leave alone.

[St. Johns County's Land Development Code, Sec. 4.01.06](https://www.sjcfl.us/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/Article-IV.pdf), sets a natural vegetative Upland Buffer between developed areas and Contiguous Wetlands — typically 25 feet, measured from the jurisdictional wetland line, and never less than 10 feet even where averaging is used. A further 25-foot setback applies behind that buffer.

The code carves out real exemptions, but they're narrower than they first sound:

- **Landscaping is exempt from the setback itself.** The code lists landscaping, fencing, a sprinkler system and similar items as uses the 25-foot setback doesn't reach, provided nothing encroaches into the buffer.
- **The buffer is not exempt from anything.** No exemption in the section lets a structure or a planted surface extend into the Upland Buffer. It's required to stay in its natural vegetated condition, and vegetation removed from it has to be restored with comparable native species.

In practice: turf can often reach into the 25-foot setback zone behind a screened lanai, right up to where the buffer starts, but the buffer line itself is where it stops. That line comes from a jurisdictional wetland determination, not a fence or a fairway edge, so it has to be located before the layout is drawn — not assumed from where the grass currently ends.

## Before you approve the layout

On the free visit we measure the enclosure and the ground under it, and check what's around it. Bring these questions to that conversation:

1. **Which safety feature does the pool use** — a fence, the screen door, or something else — so the plan protects the right one.
2. **Where does water go** once it's inside the cage, and does the grade already carry it there?
3. **What's the edge detail** where turf meets deck, pavers or the screen frame's footers, so infill stays put?
4. **Does the lot border a pond or wetland,** and if so, where is the jurisdictional buffer line?
5. **Where does the shade actually fall** at midday, since the mesh itself won't cool the surface?
