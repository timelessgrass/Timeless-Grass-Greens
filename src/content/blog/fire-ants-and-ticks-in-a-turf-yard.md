Not by itself. Turf takes away tall grass, and that helps with ticks. But fire ants nest in soil, and a surface laid over soil doesn't move them out. Ticks gather at a yard's edges, which turf doesn't reach. What works is treating fire ants before the dig and managing the edges after.

## What turf changes, and what it doesn't

A turf yard never grows tall. That removes one kind of cover. The CDC says ticks live in grassy, brushy or wooded areas, or even on animals, and its yard advice includes clearing tall grass and brush and mowing often.

What turf doesn't change:

- **The ground underneath.** Turf sits on a prepared base over your native soil. Our guide to [what goes under artificial turf](/blog/what-goes-under-artificial-turf/) walks through the layers. Fire ants live in that ground.
- **The edges.** Woods, beds, fences and brush around the turf stay as they were, and that's where ticks are.
- **Animals.** Deer, rodents and pets still cross the yard, and they can carry ticks with them.

## Fire ants nest in the ground, not the grass

Fire ants don't need grass to thrive. [UF/IFAS Extension](https://ask.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/lh059) says red imported fire ants build mounds in almost any type of soil. They prefer open, sunny places such as lawns, parks and pastures, and colonies can occur in or under buildings.

[NC State Extension](https://content.ces.ncsu.edu/fire-ants-in-turf) adds two details that matter in a turf yard:

- Mounds appear in sunny areas and next to sidewalks and driveways, where soil temperatures run higher.
- After heavy rain, the ants build mounds up to escape the rising groundwater.

Colonies also move. UF says a queen needs only about half a dozen workers to start a new colony, and they can build a new mound dozens of feet away almost overnight.

None of the extension guides we read describes a turf surface as a barrier to fire ants. Plan for the ants you have now, and for the ones that arrive later.

## Treat fire ants before the ground is dug

If you already see mounds, deal with them before the install. The ground is open and easy to reach. UF's directions for treating a single mound say not to disturb it first, and digging out a yard disturbs every mound in it.

[Clemson's Home & Garden Information Center](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/fire-ant-management-in-the-home-lawn/) recommends the Two Step Method:

1. **Broadcast a fire ant bait** over the area while the ants are actively foraging.
2. **Seven to ten days later,** treat the problem mounds that remain with an individual mound treatment.

Bait is slow on purpose. Clemson explains that it's made to act slowly enough that the queen eats it before the effects show. How slow depends on the active ingredient. UF gives these ranges:

- **Hydramethylnon, fipronil, indoxacarb, metaflumizone and spinosad baits:** maximum control 1 to 4 weeks after application.
- **Insect growth regulator baits** (methoprene or pyriproxyfen): maximum suppression 2 to 6 months after treatment.

A 2020 [Clemson fact sheet on broadcast baits](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/hints-and-tips-for-an-effective-fire-ant-management-program-in-home-landscapes-using-broadcast-baits/) says effects can take 2 to 12 weeks to show, depending on the temperature and the product. So if mounds are active, put the bait down several weeks before the dig date.

Getting the bait to the ants:

- **Check that they're foraging.** UF suggests setting a little bait near a mound and looking again 10 to 45 minutes later.
- **Watch the temperature.** UF says foraging slows when the soil is below 65 to 70°F or above 90°F. In mid-summer, spread bait in late afternoon or early evening.
- **Keep it dry.** Apply when the ground and grass are dry and no rain is expected. Clemson's 2020 sheet adds: turn off the irrigation, and wait until the dew dries.
- **Use fresh bait.** Clemson says the soybean oil in bait can go rancid, and ants won't pick it up once it does.
- **Read the label.** UF says to use only products labeled for the site you're treating.

## After the turf is down

One round doesn't end it. UF says bait doesn't protect against colonies moving in from surrounding land or against newly mated queens. Populations can fully recover within 12 to 18 months of the last bait treatment.

Those queens travel. Clemson says a newly mated female can fly as far as 2 to 3 miles before landing, and calls fire ant control a long-term commitment.

Its 2020 sheet recommends two bait applications a year: one in late May or early June, and one in late September or early October. Before you spread anything over the turf itself, confirm the label covers that site.

Between treatments, walk the edges. Look along the driveway, walks, patio and border beds, the warm, sunny spots where NC State says mounds tend to appear. Look again after a heavy rain.

## Ticks gather at the edges

Ticks aren't spread evenly across a yard. The [Connecticut Agricultural Experiment Station's Tick Management Handbook](https://portal.ct.gov/-/media/caes/documents/publications/bulletins/b1010pdf.pdf), revised in 2007, reports where blacklegged ticks were found on home properties:

- **Dense woods:** 67% of the ticks sampled.
- **The unmaintained strip between woods and open ground:** 22%.
- **Ornamental plantings:** 9%.
- **Lawn:** 2%.

Within the lawn, 82% were within 3 yards of its edge, along woods, stone walls or plantings. That work covered one tick species in Connecticut, not our areas, but the pattern is worth borrowing. The middle of an open, sunny yard is seldom where ticks are. The handbook says bright, sunny areas are less likely to harbor ticks, and that shade and tree canopy affect how many turn up on a lawn.

So turf helps most where it replaces shaggy, shaded ground, and changes little at the border. For the border, the CDC lists:

- Remove leaf litter.
- Clear tall grass and brush around the house and at the edge of the lawn.
- Place a 3-foot-wide barrier of wood chips or gravel between the lawn and wooded areas.
- Stack wood neatly in a dry spot, which discourages rodents.
- Keep play equipment, decks and patios away from yard edges and trees.

The handbook is measured about that barrier. It may help reduce ticks on the lawn, but results vary widely from home to home and year to year. It also warns that wood chips with leaves in them break down and soon become no different from leaf litter. Gravel doesn't rot. For clearing leaves off the turf itself, see [leaves and pine needles on artificial turf](/blog/leaves-and-pine-needles-on-artificial-turf/).

## Dogs and stings

A turf yard won't keep ticks off a dog. The CDC's advice for pets is to check pets who go outdoors every day, use a tick preventive product, and talk to your veterinarian. It warns that pets can bring ticks inside, where they may bite people.

Fire ants matter for dogs too. NC State says their stings can cause serious pain and injury to people, pets and other animals. If you're planning [pet turf](/services/pet-turf/), treat the mounds first, and follow the bait label on keeping pets off treated ground.

## Planning the layout around both

On the free visit we measure the space and check the ground. Tell us where you've seen mounds, and where the yard meets woods or brush. The custom layout you approve can then:

- set play areas and dog runs away from wooded edges and heavy shade,

## Before and after the install

- Look for mounds well before the install date.
- Broadcast a labeled fire ant bait on a dry day while the ants are foraging.
- Treat leftover mounds seven to ten days later.
- Set the dig date after the bait has had time to work.
- Bait again in late spring and early fall.
- Walk the edges along paving, and again after heavy rain.
- Keep a 3-foot gravel strip where turf meets woods.
- Rake leaf litter and clear brush at the border.
- Check your dog for ticks every day.
