## Yes, if the slope is gentle and the water has a way off

Artificial turf can follow a slope. The carpet doesn't mind the angle. What sits under it does.

On flat ground, rain goes down through the turf and base and into the soil. On a slope, water that isn't soaking in keeps moving downhill, across the surface and through the stone. Loose infill can go with it. And the bottom edge of your lawn is often the top of someone else's yard.

Three questions decide whether turf belongs on your slope:

1. **Will the soil and base stay put?**
2. **Will the infill stay on the turf?**
3. **Where does the water end up?**

If all three have good answers, turf can work. If the bank is steep, a terrace or a planted slope may do the job better. How water passes through each layer on level ground is covered in [does rain drain through artificial turf](/blog/does-rain-drain-through-artificial-turf/).

## Why a slope sheds more water under turf

A living lawn has roots. Turf doesn't, and on a slope that difference shows.

[UF/IFAS Extension](https://ask.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/EP612) says a living lawn's root zone "helps to filter and slow runoff and stop erosion." Its authors describe a typical home install as stripping 2 to 3 inches of topsoil and heavily compacting the rest. That compaction, they write, "reduces soil infiltration rates and increases the risk of runoff." They also point to a 2021 rainfall study in which synthetic lawns shed more water than living ones.

None of that rules turf out. It means two things on a slope:

- **The soil can't be packed shut.** Florida's rule for home lawns bars compacting the soil under the base to the point that water can no longer percolate ([Rule 62-308.100](https://flrules.org/gateway/readFile.asp?sid=0&tid=30839130&type=1&file=62-308.100.doc)).
- **The bottom of the slope needs a plan.** Water that runs through the base arrives at the low edge. Something there has to take it: a pervious bed, a swale on your own lot, or a drain.

## Keeping infill and edges in place

Infill is the sand brushed down between the blades. It weighs the turf down and holds the fibers upright. On a slope, water moving across the surface can carry it off.

- **Light infill floats.** [New Jersey's environment department](https://dep.nj.gov/wp-content/uploads/dsr/synthetic-turf-report-2025.pdf) warns that rubber pellets are buoyant and may wash away. Where heavy rain is common, it points to infill with high specific gravity, such as coated sand or zeolite, as less likely to move.
- **Saturated infill migrates.** The [Synthetic Turf Council's suggested guidelines](https://cdn.ymaws.com/staging-stc.site-ym.com/resource/resmgr/guidelines/STC_Suggested_Guidelines_for.pdf), revised in November 2011, discuss turf over sloped pavement. Without a drainage layer, they say, infill saturates in heavy rain and can migrate with the surface water. Their answer is a drainage mat or pad under the turf, plus interceptor drains, so water can move sideways below the surface.
- **Florida puts it in writing.** The state rule requires a home install to be designed to keep infill from washing off the property. It also requires anchoring at all edges and seams strong enough to withstand wind or flooding.

For the edges themselves, the council's guidelines list a concrete curb, a treated wood header, a composite material or a trench drain, and say each "should always provide a secure anchor." At the foot of a slope, a trench drain can hold the edge and catch the water in one.

## Where the water goes

The low edge is where a slope becomes someone else's problem. It's also where the written rules focus.

**Northeast Florida.** Since 19 May 2026, Rule 62-308.100 has set statewide minimum standards for turf on single-family lots of 1 acre or less. An install must be designed to prevent pooling, or an increase in the volume, direction or rate of runoff to adjacent properties. Where possible, runoff goes to pervious ground on your own lot. Turf can't go in a swale, ditch or stormwater pond at all.

[Atlantic Beach's guidelines](https://coab.us/DocumentCenter/View/15920/Turf-Installation-Guidelines) say it plainly: turf must not interfere with existing drainage patterns "or cause runoff onto adjacent properties." The city asks for a site plan showing the turf against property lines, and inspects the stone base before the turf goes down.

**Denver metro.** Here the house below matters as much as the neighbor. The [2021 International Residential Code, as published for Colorado](https://up.codes/viewer/colorado/irc-2021/chapter/4/foundations), requires lots to drain water away from foundation walls, with the grade falling at least 6 inches in the first 10 feet. Surface water has to go to an approved point that does not create a hazard. Where lot lines or slopes make that fall impossible, drains or swales take its place.

The [Colorado Geological Survey](https://coloradogeologicalsurvey.org/wp-content/uploads/woocommerce_uploads/SP-14.pdf) adds a caution for swelling soil: water channeled away from one house should not be sent against a neighboring one. If your yard already tilts toward a house, turf won't change which way the water runs.

**Everywhere else,** including the Grand Strand, ask your town or county stormwater or building office what applies before a slope is regraded. Whether turf counts toward a lot's impervious limit is a separate question, covered in our guide to [whether artificial turf is impervious](/guides/is-artificial-turf-impervious/).

## How steep is too steep?

No one publishes a single limit for turf. These are the reference points in the sources:

- **5 percent** (6 inches of fall in 10 feet) is the code's minimum fall away from a foundation.
- **10 percent** (1 foot in 10 feet) is the Colorado Geological Survey's recommended grade next to a house.
- **15 percent** is where the Survey stops recommending slopes near a house on swelling soil, because steep slopes "result in downhill movement and erosion of the soil."
- **33.3 percent** (1 foot of rise in 3 feet) is where the code starts setting special rules for buildings on or next to a slope, to protect against "slope drainage, erosion and shallow failures." In Virginia, the [Fairfax County Soil and Water Conservation District](https://www.fairfaxcounty.gov/soil-water-conservation/drainage-problem-protect-eroding-land) notes that slopes this steep may call for tiered walls that need engineering and a building permit there.

To find your own number, measure the drop from top to bottom and the level distance between those points. Divide the drop by the distance. A 2-foot drop over 20 feet is 10 percent.

## Turf, a terrace or planting?

**Choose turf** when the slope is gentle, you'll walk or play on it, and water has a safe way off at the bottom.

**Choose a terrace** when the bank is steep but you want ground you can use. Walls turn one slope into a series of near-level steps, and each step can take turf. The Fairfax district says shorter terraces in series work better than tall ones, with gravel or perforated pipe behind each wall. Under the 2021 code, a wall holding back more than 48 inches of unbalanced fill must be designed to accepted engineering practice. Your building department decides which permits apply.

**Choose planting** for a bank no one uses, or one that runs down to water. UF/IFAS says turf along the edge of a water body "increases the risk of soil erosion" because it has no root zone to hold the soil. In Florida, unless a local buffer applies, the state rule keeps turf at least 10 feet from a natural or man-made waterbody, except behind a seawall or bulkhead.

You can mix them: turf on the flat, a wall where the grade breaks, and planting on the bank.

## Before you decide

1. **Measure the fall** from top to bottom and work out the percentage.
2. **Watch a heavy rain.** Note where water crosses your lot and where it leaves.
3. **Look downhill.** Is there a house, a neighbor's yard, a swale, a pond or open water at the bottom?
4. **Ask your city or county** what applies to runoff, regrading and walls. In Florida, start with Rule 62-308.100.
5. **Ask how the infill and edges will hold,** and where the bottom edge sends water.
6. **Pick a surface for each part of the slope:** turf, terrace or planting.
