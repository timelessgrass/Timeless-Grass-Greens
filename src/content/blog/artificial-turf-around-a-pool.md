## Start with the pool barrier, not the turf

Turf can sit right beside a pool deck. The layout just has to work around three things: the barrier that keeps small children out, the water that leaves the pool, and the heat a synthetic surface holds in the sun.

The barrier comes first, because it is the one piece with a legal standard behind it.

In Florida, a new residential pool must have at least one of five safety features to pass final inspection ([Fla. Stat. 515.27](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2025/515.27)). A barrier is one. The others are an approved safety cover, exit alarms on doors and windows to the pool, self-closing and self-latching doors, or a pool alarm. If your pool relies on a barrier, the turf has to leave that barrier working.

## What Florida's barrier law asks for

[Fla. Stat. 515.29](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2025/515.29), in the 2025 Florida Statutes, sets out the barrier requirements. In short:

- **Height.** At least 4 feet high on the outside.
- **No way through, under or over.** No gaps, openings, indentations, protrusions or structural parts that would let a young child crawl under, squeeze through or climb over.
- **Distance from the water.** Far enough from the water's edge that a child who gets past it doesn't fall straight in.
- **Gates.** They open outward, away from the pool, close by themselves and latch by themselves.
- **Nothing to climb.** The barrier may not sit where "any permanent structure, equipment, or similar object" can be used to climb it.

A "young child" in this law means anyone under 6 ([Fla. Stat. 515.25](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2025/515.25)). This is a summary, not legal advice. The building department that inspects your pool applies the rule, so take layout questions to that office.

## Four ways a turf job can change the barrier

Turf is flat, so it rarely becomes the problem itself. What changes is the ground and the things placed on it.

**1. The finished height.** The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission's [barrier guidelines](https://www.cpsc.gov/s3fs-public/362%20Safety%20Barrier%20Guidelines%20for%20Pools.pdf) measure the top of the barrier "above grade, measured on the side of the barrier which faces away from the swimming pool." If the new surface outside the fence ends up higher than the ground there today, the fence is shorter than it was. Measure from where the finished turf will sit.

**2. The gap underneath.** The same guidelines allow up to 4 inches between the surface and the bottom of the barrier. Over a non-solid surface, such as grass or pebbles, they cut that to 2 inches. Ask your building department which figure it applies where turf meets the fence.

**3. Things to climb.** Boulders, raised planters, a bench, a mounded edge on a putting green or the pool equipment pad can all turn into a step. Keep them clear of the outside of the barrier.

**4. The gates.** Once the turf is down, open each gate and let go. It should still swing shut and latch on its own, without dragging on the new surface.

## Outside Florida

Florida's statute doesn't reach the Grand Strand or the Denver metro. There, the barrier rules for your pool come from the code your local building department applies.

The CPSC guidelines are a useful reference anywhere. They note that some states, counties or municipalities require 60-inch barriers, not 48, and they point readers to the local building department or code authority. Ask that office before the layout is final.

## Splash water and deck drainage

A pool puts water on the ground beyond the rain: splash-out, wet feet, the deck being hosed down. Decide where that water goes before the turf goes in.

Look at which way the deck slopes. If it sheds water toward the lawn, the turf and its base take that water on top of every storm.

Florida's statewide turf standard, [Rule 62-308.100](https://flrules.org/gateway/readFile.asp?sid=0&tid=30839130&type=1&file=62-308.100.doc), in force since 19 May 2026, covers single-family properties of 1 acre or less. It requires:

- **Permeable turf** on a permeable backing, over a subgrade prepared for positive drainage.
- **No pooling,** and no increase in runoff volume, direction or rate onto neighboring properties. Where possible, runoff goes to pervious areas on your own lot.
- **Natural infill** such as clean silica sand, with the job designed so infill doesn't wash off the property. Our [Florida infill guide](/guides/florida-turf-infill-rules/) sets that rule out in full.

Sand that washes or gets kicked onto the deck can end up in the pool. A firm, well-anchored edge where the turf meets the coping helps keep it on the lawn.

## Chlorine and pool chemicals

Splashes of pool water are not the main concern. The Synthetic Turf Council's 2013 [buyer guide](https://cdn.ymaws.com/www.syntheticturfcouncil.org/resource/resmgr/guidelines/STC_Considerations_When_Buyi.pdf) says polyethylene, nylon and polypropylene yarns can all resist stains and chemicals, and names chlorinated pool water "at safe concentrations" in that list.

Two things follow from that wording:

- **Ask for the data.** The same guide says yarn properties should be backed by a report from an independent test lab. Ask for it for the product you are choosing.
- **Keep the bucket off the grass.** Swimming-strength water is one thing. Chemicals as they come from the container are another. Mix and store them on the deck or at the equipment pad, not on the turf.

## Heat beside the water

People walk barefoot around a pool, which makes surface heat matter more here than almost anywhere in the yard.

[Penn State researchers](https://plantscience.psu.edu/research/centers/ssrc/documents/temperature-irrigation.pdf) measured ten synthetic sports turf systems in Pennsylvania. On 3 August 2004, the surfaces read between 53.8°C and 71.5°C (about 129°F to 161°F), while the air a meter above sat between 28.3°C and 30.8°C.

Water helps, but not for long at full strength. After about 2 cm (roughly three-quarters of an inch) of water, surface temperatures dropped sharply, then "rebounded somewhat after 15 minutes" before leveling off. On one day, 200 minutes later, the watered plots still averaged 14 degrees Celsius cooler than the dry ones. The researchers conclude that the benefit "will vary depending on conditions."

A splash from the pool is far less water than that. Our article on [how hot artificial turf gets](/blog/how-hot-does-artificial-turf-get/) covers the rest of the research.

In Florida, the state rule says in-ground irrigation systems cannot be used to irrigate synthetic turf areas. Where a system already exists, your local government may require the heads removed and the pipe capped. Plan for heat with the layout, not with sprinklers.

That means:

- **Keep the barefoot route on the deck or in shade.** The path from the back door to the pool steps matters more than the lawn's far corner.
- **Put the turf where the afternoon shade falls,** and leave the sunniest strip beside the water as deck.
- **Give people somewhere to stand** that isn't turf, near the steps and the shower.

## Before you approve the layout

On the free visit we measure the space and check the ground. Then we draw a [custom turf layout](/services/residential-turf/) for you to approve before any work starts. Bring these to that conversation:

1. **Which safety feature did your pool pass inspection with?** If it's the barrier, the turf plan works around it.
2. **Measure the barrier from the outside,** from where the finished turf surface will be.
3. **Check the gap under the fence** against the figure your building department uses.
4. **Clear anything climbable** away from the outside of the barrier: boulders, planters, benches, green mounds, equipment.
5. **Test every gate** after the turf is down. It must close and latch by itself.
6. **Mark where deck water and splash-out drain,** and keep that path open.
7. **Ask for the yarn's lab data,** and keep pool chemicals off the turf.
8. **Map the shade and the barefoot route** before deciding where the turf ends and the deck begins.
