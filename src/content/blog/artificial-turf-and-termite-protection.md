## The short answer

Turf and termite protection can conflict at exactly one spot: the strip of soil against your foundation. If a pest control company has treated that soil, or set bait stations along it, digging a turf base through that strip can disturb the barrier. It doesn't rule turf out. It means that stretch needs a conversation with your pest control company before anyone breaks ground.

Everywhere else in the yard, a turf install has nothing to do with termite protection. This is specifically about the few feet closest to the house.

## What a soil barrier actually is

Most subterranean termite treatments in Florida and coastal South Carolina rely on one of two approaches, the [Florida Department of Agriculture and Consumer Services](https://www.fdacs.gov/Consumer-Resources/Health-and-Safety/Protect-Your-Home-from-Pests/Termites/Subterranean-Termites) (FDACS) notes: a non-repellent chemical soil barrier, or an in-ground bait system. A soil barrier is termiticide applied to the ground around and under the foundation, sometimes injected along the base of the walls. It works by staying in place in the soil. Move the soil, and you move the barrier, or break its continuity.

FDACS is direct about the result. Its guidance to homeowners is simple: don't disturb the termite treatment around your foundation.

Turf installation involves exactly the kind of work that disturbs it. A proper base means grading, sometimes excavating a few inches of soil, and compacting aggregate before the turf and infill go down. Do that along a wall with a treated barrier underneath, and you've dug through it.

## Disturbed soil may need retreating

This isn't a guess about what might happen. [UF/IFAS Extension's termite prevention publication](https://ask.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/IN1277) (ENY-2044/IN1277) addresses disturbed treated soil directly:

> "If the treated soil around your home is disturbed, for instance, by excavations for construction, digging by pets or wildlife, or a washing rain such as may be experienced during a hurricane, be aware that those areas may require another treatment."

Notice what's on that list: construction excavation sits right alongside storm damage as a cause of retreatment. A turf base dug against the foundation is construction excavation. The publication doesn't carve out an exception for landscaping work, and there's no reason to assume termiticide treats a turf base differently than a planting bed or a footing.

The practical result: if your foundation has a soil barrier, plan on the treated zone needing another pass after excavation, not before it. Loop your pest control company in before the shovel goes in the ground, not after.

## The 6-inch inspection gap still applies

UF/IFAS Extension sets a second rule that a turf layout by the house has to respect, regardless of whether termiticide is involved: an inspection space.

> "Keep an inspection space of at least 6 inches from the bottom of wall sidings and the soil or mulch line."

That gap exists so a technician, or you, can see the foundation wall itself. Subterranean termites build visible mud tubes across exposed concrete or block to reach wood framing above. Cover that strip with mulch, soil, or turf brought up flush against the siding, and you hide the one sign that would tell anyone termites are active.

The same publication is specific that this isn't only about not planting too close: "Avoid creating hidden access by burying exterior wall coverings with soil or mulch." A turf edge is a wall covering in the same sense a flower bed is. If the base and infill bring the finished surface up to the bottom of the siding, the inspection gap is gone even though nothing was "planted."

Our [guide to how close turf can go to your house](/blog/artificial-turf-next-to-the-house/) covers the separate building-code and drainage rules for that same strip — the two sets of requirements have to be met together, not traded off against each other.

## Point irrigation away from the wall

Termites are drawn to moisture, and a foundation with damp soil against it is more attractive than a dry one. UF/IFAS Extension's advice here is short: "Direct irrigation heads away from walls." It adds a related rule for anything that holds standing water: keep rain barrels, compost bins, and similar items at least 2 feet from the foundation.

If turf is going in near the house, this is worth building into the layout from the start, not fixing afterward. A turf lawn's own drainage — [what goes under artificial turf](/blog/what-goes-under-artificial-turf/) covers the base and drainage layers — should already be routing water away from the wall on its own. The irrigation heads that water anything else nearby (planting beds, adjacent lawn) should be aimed the same direction.

## Bait stations sit exactly where the base work happens

An in-ground bait system is the other treatment FDACS describes. Stations are typically installed in the soil around the perimeter of the house, often close to the foundation — the same strip a turf base would run through.

A station in the way isn't a reason to skip that section of yard. It's a reason to ask your pest control company two questions before turf work starts there: where each station sits, and whether it needs to be moved, capped, or worked around. Digging into a station without knowing it's there can damage it or interrupt monitoring that's been running for months or years.

## Keep mulch and plant material off the foundation, too

FDACS pairs its treatment guidance with a related instruction: keep mulch and plant material away from the exterior and foundation of the home. Mulch holds moisture against the wall and can itself bridge the inspection gap the same way buried turf edging can.

If the plan is turf right up to the house, that FDACS guidance is worth applying to the turf edge, too — not stopping the material flush against the siding, but leaving the inspection strip FDACS and UF/IFAS both describe.

## Before excavation starts

1. **Ask if the foundation has an active termite treatment** — a soil barrier, bait stations, or both — and where the treated zone or stations run.
2. **Call your pest control company before digging** along the foundation, not after. Ask whether the area will need retreating once base work disturbs it.
3. **Keep 6 inches clear** between the bottom of the siding and the finished turf or infill line, so the inspection gap FDACS and UF/IFAS describe stays visible.
4. **Point irrigation heads away from the wall,** and keep anything that holds water at least 2 feet off the foundation.
5. **Get the retreatment conversation in writing** if soil near the foundation will be excavated, so there's no dispute afterward about whether it was disturbed.
