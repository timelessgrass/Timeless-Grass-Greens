## The court is 20 by 44 feet. Your yard needs more than that.

Start with the number everyone quotes and stops at. The [USA Pickleball Official Rulebook](https://www.usapickleball.org/docs/rules/USAP-Official-Rulebook-v2025.pdf) sets the court itself at 20 feet wide by 44 feet long, for singles and doubles alike. That's the painted rectangle.

It is not the space you need to actually play on it. The same rulebook sets a minimum playing surface of 30 feet by 60 feet, so a player chasing a ball doesn't run off the edge of the surface mid-point. A full 10-foot margin beyond the court lines on every side works out to 40 by 64 feet. For new construction, the rulebook recommends more: 34 feet by 64 feet. Tournament play uses the same 34-by-64 figure; a wheelchair court needs 44 by 74.

That gap between "the court" and "the space" is where most backyard plans go wrong. A homeowner measures 20 by 44, finds it fits, and only later realizes there's nowhere to stand for a backhand near the baseline.

## The lines that decide where everything else goes

Two more numbers from the rulebook matter once you're laying out the yard around the court, not just the court.

The non-volley zone — the "kitchen" — runs 7 feet back from the net on each side. That's fixed geometry: it sets where the net line sits relative to the baselines no matter how the court is oriented on your lot.

Net height is measured two ways: 36 inches at the point where the net crosses each sideline, and 34 inches at the center, where a strap can hold it down. Neither number changes how much ground you need, but both matter to whoever pours the pad and sets the posts, since the base has to hold that line true.

## The court isn't turf. What's around it can be.

A pickleball court needs a rigid, dead-flat playing surface — concrete, asphalt, or a modular tile system over one of those. That's a paving contractor's scope, not ours. We don't pour or top-coat a court.

What we build is the ground around it. The buffer inside that 30-by-60 or 34-by-64 footprint, past the hard pad, is where turf does real work: a consistent, mowable, drainable surface a player can step onto without catching a foot on grass ruts or bare dirt at the edge of the pad. It also carries the rest of the yard up to the court without a visible seam between "lawn" and "court."

That's the scope of our free visit: we measure the full footprint, not just the court, and check how the ground around the pad drains before anything gets built.

## Questions to settle before the layout is final

**Where does the 34-by-64 footprint actually fit?** Walk your property line, setbacks and any easements before committing to an orientation. A footprint that fits on paper can still crowd a fence line or a drainage swale once you measure it on the ground.

**What does your community's governing document say — specifically?** Not every HOA addresses sport courts by name, and the ones that do can surprise you. St. Johns Golf and Country Club's own [Architectural Guidelines](https://stjohnsgcc.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/11/AdoptedARB-Guidelines-2010.pdf) don't ban courts outright, but Section 7 bars exterior lighting "for the purpose of illuminating basketball play areas or other exterior play equipment," aside from normal landscape and architectural lighting. A homeowner planning evening play would run straight into that rule. Read your own community's current guidelines before you design around lights, fencing or a dedicated court at all — don't assume a rule written for one type of play area doesn't reach another.

**What if the documents are silent?** Ask the board in writing how a court request will be reviewed. If a community wants to formalize a rule rather than leave it to board discretion, [Fla. Stat. 720.306(1)(b)](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2025/720.306) sets the standard path in Florida: an amendment to the governing documents takes the affirmative vote of two-thirds of the voting interests of the association, then gets recorded.

**Where does water go once there's a hard pad in the yard?** A rigid, sloped court surface sheds rain somewhere. Know which way before the turf around it goes in, so runoff has a place to land instead of pooling at the pad's edge.
