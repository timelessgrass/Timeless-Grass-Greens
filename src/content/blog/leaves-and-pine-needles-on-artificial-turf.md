Clear leaves and needles off artificial turf before they settle in. Under trees in heavy drop, that means about once a week. A blower on low or a soft rake does it, as long as the infill stays behind.

## What leaves do when they stay

A leaf left on turf doesn't stay on top. It works down between the blades and starts to break down.

The [Synthetic Turf Council](https://cdn.ymaws.com/staging-stc.site-ym.com/resource/resmgr/guidelines/STC_Guidelines_for_Maintenan.pdf) says leaves and other debris left on the surface migrate into the turf system. Once there, they slow drainage and compact the infill.

The UK sports surface builders' [code of practice](https://sapca.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/SAPCA_Code_of_Practice_Construction_Maintenance_Synthetic_Turf_01.pdf), from SAPCA, names pine needles and tree flowers alongside leaves. Left in place, it says, they decay fast and form a layer in the surface that blocks drainage and feeds moss and algae.

Home lawns work the same way. The [UC Master Gardeners](https://ucanr.edu/blog/over-fence-alameda-county/article/artificial-turf-what-consider) warn that leaves that aren't cleared get ground into artificial grass. [UF/IFAS Extension](https://ask.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/EP612) says fallen leaves still have to come off synthetic turf to prevent wear and tear. It puts turf's life at perhaps 10 to 20 years, depending on ongoing care that includes removing leaves.

One sign the debris has already moved in: water that stands after a heavy rain. The Synthetic Turf Council lists debris in the infill among the possible causes of ponding. If moss has followed, see [moss and algae on artificial turf](/blog/moss-and-algae-on-artificial-turf/).

## When the trees drop

Your leaf season depends on the trees over the turf, not only the calendar.

- **Pines.** Clemson's [Home & Garden Information Center](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/leaf-needle-drop/) says most pines drop their needles in the fall, though some species shed at other times. The older needles yellow and fall, while the needles at the branch tips stay green. Clemson calls it a normal part of the tree's growth cycle.
- **Holly and Southern magnolia.** The same fact sheet says many broad-leaved evergreens, these two among them, drop many of their oldest leaves in spring as new growth begins.
- **Southern live oak.** [UF/IFAS](https://ask.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/ST564) lists it as semi-evergreen to evergreen, and flags its fruit and leaves as a litter problem.

So a yard with pines and magnolias can have two heavy seasons, fall and spring. Watch your own trees for a year and you'll know when to step up.

## How often to clear it

Often enough that nothing sits. SAPCA says leaves, needles and tree flowers shouldn't stay on the surface for any length of time. The UC Master Gardeners suggest cleaning, rinsing and brushing weekly or monthly.

A rhythm that fits most yards with trees:

- **During heavy drop,** clear the turf under and beside the trees about once a week, plus after any storm that brings a lot down at once.
- **The rest of the year,** fold it into your regular brushing.
- **In shade, do more.** SAPCA treats grooming once a week as the norm for a pitch, and more often where it's shaded. The Synthetic Turf Council notes that moss, mold and algae tend to show up in shaded, damp areas that see little use.

Look where the wind leaves things: corners, fence lines, the edges of beds and the lip of a patio. Needles are thin, so check down between the blades, not just the surface.

## Blower or rake: keeping the infill in place

Both work. The risk with either is lifting the infill out with the leaves.

Infill is the sand or other granules brushed down between the blades. [Penn State's Center for Sports Surface Research](https://plantscience.psu.edu/research/centers/ssrc/documents/ftfsynmaint1.pdf/@@download/file/FTFsynmaint1.pdf) says it helps keep the fibers upright and gives the surface its cushion. The same researchers say leaves can come off with sweepers or blowers, as long as you don't blow out or remove the infill.

**With a blower:**

- Keep the throttle low. For blowing small debris off a field, the Synthetic Turf Council says to use minimal throttle and not to point the nozzle directly into the turf.
- Work from one side toward a single pile, instead of chasing leaves back and forth.
- Move the pile onto a hard surface or a tarp, then bag it.

**With a rake or broom:**

- SAPCA calls a wide soft broom or a rubber-tined rake ideal for lifting leaves and other plant matter.
- Skip metal. The Synthetic Turf Council says not to use metal or wire bristles.
- Brush the blades back up afterward. The council says brushing keeps fibers upright and infill levels even, and works better when the surface is dry.

Two cautions. SAPCA says mechanical sweepers and vacuums can pull out too much infill in the first few months of a new surface, though that should stop being a problem after. And an October 2025 [report from New Jersey's Department of Environmental Protection](https://dep.nj.gov/wp-content/uploads/dsr/synthetic-turf-report-2025.pdf) blames most of the plastic particles leaving turf fields on maintenance habits, naming leaf blowing, as opposed to raking, among them. Rake where you can, and don't blow the pile into the street, where any infill that went with it washes away.

## On a putting green

A single needle on a green sits in the line of a putt, so clear the surface before you play. Use the same gentle tools: a soft broom, or a blower on low. Our [putting green care guide](/blog/putting-green-care/) covers the rest of the routine.

## Leaf litter and ticks

The leaves matter past the edge of the turf. The CDC's [tick bite prevention page](https://www.cdc.gov/ticks/prevention/index.html), last updated in August 2024, says ticks live in grassy, brushy or wooded areas. It lists removing leaf litter among the landscaping steps that reduce blacklegged ticks, along with clearing tall grass and brush around homes and at the edge of lawns. It also advises keeping play equipment, decks and patios away from yard edges and trees.

The CDC's [Lyme disease prevention page](https://www.cdc.gov/lyme/prevention/index.html), updated in May 2026, adds two steps that fit a turf yard:

- **Let in light.** Trim tree branches and shrubs. The CDC says ticks are less likely to be found in bright, sunny areas.
- **Put a buffer at a wooded edge.** A 3-foot strip of wood chips or gravel between the lawn and wooded areas discourages contact with brushy borders.

So clear the turf, then deal with the pile. Leaving it against the back fence puts the leaf litter right where the CDC says not to have it.

## Planning turf under trees

For sports fields, the Synthetic Turf Council says leafy trees shouldn't be next to the field where that can be avoided. In a back yard you're usually keeping the tree, so the layout does the work:

- **Set play areas and putting cups out from under the canopy,** away from the yard's edges and trees, as the CDC advises for play equipment.
- **Leave room to rake.** A tight gap behind a trunk or a shed collects leaves you can't reach.
- **Plan the wooded edge.** Where turf meets trees or brush, a strip of gravel or wood chips gives you the buffer the CDC describes.

## A leaf-season checklist

- Learn when your trees drop: most pines in fall, holly and magnolia in spring.
- Clear under the trees about weekly during heavy drop, and after storms.
- Blow on low throttle, never straight into the turf, or use a soft broom or rubber-tined rake.
- No metal or wire bristles.
- Bag the pile. Keep it out of the street and away from the fence line.
- Brush the blades back up and look for thin spots in the infill.
- After a heavy rain, check for standing water.
- Trim low limbs so light reaches the turf and the edges of the yard.
