## Where turf could sit in a homeowners policy

The Insurance Information Institute publishes a [sample of the HO-3 special form](https://www.iii.org/sites/default/files/docs/pdf/HO3_sample.pdf) written by Insurance Services Office (ISO), edition HO 00 03 10 00. Your insurer may use that wording, a later edition or its own, so treat this as a guide to where to look in your policy. It is general information, not insurance advice: your policy and its declarations page decide what is covered.

The form never names artificial turf. Three parts of it could reach a turf lawn:

- **Trees, shrubs, plants or lawns**, an extra landscaping coverage paid on top of the dwelling limit.
- **Other structures (Coverage B)**: structures "set apart from the dwelling by clear space", including ones joined to the house only by a fence, a utility line or similar.
- **The dwelling (Coverage A)**, "including structures attached to the dwelling".

## The lawn coverage: seven causes and one shared limit

The sample form covers "trees, shrubs, plants or lawns" for loss from seven causes only:

1. Fire or lightning
2. Explosion
3. Riot or civil commotion
4. Aircraft
5. Vehicles not owned or driven by someone who lives there
6. Vandalism or malicious mischief
7. Theft

Wind and hail are not on the list.

The limit is shared by the whole yard: up to 5% of the dwelling limit for all trees, shrubs, plants and lawns together. With $300,000 of dwelling coverage, that is $15,000 for everything growing on the lot. Any one tree, shrub or plant is capped at $500; that sentence does not mention lawns. The [Insurance Information Institute](https://www.iii.org/article/what-is-covered-by-standard-homeowners-insurance) adds that trees and plants are not covered for disease, or if they have been poorly maintained.

The form does not say whether a synthetic lawn counts as a "lawn". If your insurer reads it that way, a fire, a vandal or a car that isn't yours would be covered causes. A hailstorm or a hurricane would not be.

## Other structures: broader causes, its own limit

Coverages A and B work the other way round. They cover "risk of direct physical loss" and then list what is excluded.

Coverage B on the sample form is limited to 10% of the dwelling limit. The [North Carolina Department of Insurance](https://www.ncdoi.gov/consumers/homeowners-insurance/basic-homeowners-insurance) says that 10% is normal, that you may buy more, and that the policy pays less if Coverage B falls below 80% of the full replacement cost of your other structures. The fence, the shed and any detached garage share that limit, so if your insurer counts turf as an other structure, it adds to what that limit has to cover.

Four exclusions under Coverages A and B matter for a turf yard:

- **Wear and tear, marring and deterioration.** The Insurance Information Institute says a standard policy will not pay for routine wear and tear, so turf that has simply worn out is a care cost to plan for.
- **Animals owned or kept by the household, and birds, vermin, rodents or insects.** That takes in your own dog and the diggers in our guide to [gophers and voles under turf](/blog/gophers-and-voles-under-artificial-turf/).
- **Settling, shrinking, bulging or expansion** of pavements, patios, footings, foundations and walls.
- **Water damage**, which includes flood, surface water, waves and tidal water, "whether or not driven by wind".

## Hail in the Denver metro

Hail is where the two readings split. Under the lawn coverage, hail is not a covered cause. Under other structures it is not excluded, so the deductible becomes the question.

The [Colorado Division of Insurance](https://content.govdelivery.com/accounts/CODORA/bulletins/3e68963) says many homeowners policies carry wind and hail deductibles set as a percentage of coverage, for example 1%, 2% or 5%, and that the insurer will not issue a payment if the damage estimate is below the deductible. Find that deductible in dollars on your declarations page before you count on a payment for a turf repair.

After a hailstorm, the Division advises photographing the damage once it is safe, contacting your insurer or agent promptly, and asking the adjuster for an itemized explanation of any settlement offer. If you disagree, ask for the specific policy language in question, and get any denial in writing. It also notes that repairs for damage that was not part of the storm will not be covered.

Ground that moved over the winter is a separate problem. Our guide to [turf in a Colorado winter](/blog/does-artificial-turf-hold-up-in-colorado-winters/) covers what freeze, thaw and swelling clay do under a lawn, and why the base is crushed rock.

## Wind, hurricanes and flood on the coast

On the Grand Strand and in Northeast Florida, first find out whether your homeowners policy covers wind at all.

- **North Carolina.** The [NC Department of Insurance](https://www.ncdoi.gov/consumers/homeowners-insurance/windstorm-and-hail) says windstorm and hail may be excluded from the main policy, depending on where you live and the insurer's rules. A separate wind and hail policy, often from the NC Insurance Underwriting Association (the Coastal Property Insurance Pool), has its own deductible. Named storm deductibles are usually a percentage of the dwelling or personal property limit: 2% of $300,000 is $6,000.
- **South Carolina.** The [SC Department of Insurance](https://www.doi.sc.gov/847/Hurricane-Preparedness) points readers to the SC Wind and Hail Underwriting Association, the SC Wind Pool. It warns that once a storm is named and likely to hit, insurers typically stop issuing new policies and changing existing ones, so settle coverage before the season.
- **Florida.** The [Department of Financial Services](https://www.myfloridacfo.com/division/consumers/consumerprotections/floridashurricanedeductible) says insurers must offer hurricane deductible options of $500, 2%, 5% or 10% of the dwelling or structure limits, with some exceptions at higher limits. The deductible applies once per calendar year with the same insurer or group, and the Department tells policyholders to file windstorm claims even when the repair costs less than the deductible, so the amount is credited.

Flood is its own policy in all three states. The SC Department of Insurance says the standard homeowners policy does not cover flood, including storm surge, and that a new National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP) policy has a 30-day waiting period. Florida's definition of hurricane coverage leaves out flooding too.

The federal flood policy will not pick up the turf either. The NFIP's [Standard Flood Insurance Policy dwelling form](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/44/appendix-A%281%29_to_part_61) does not cover "lawns, trees, shrubs, plants" or "walks, walkways, decks, driveways, patios and other surfaces" outside the exterior walls of the building. Whether your turf counts as a lawn or as an outdoor surface, both are on that list. A private flood policy can be written differently, so read its exclusions.

For the turf itself, see our [hurricane guide for turf](/blog/artificial-turf-hurricane-prep/). If your lot is in a mapped flood zone, the [flood zone permit guide](/guides/flood-zone-artificial-turf-permits/) covers the ground work.

## What to ask your agent

Ask in writing, so the answer stays with your policy. The NC Department of Insurance suggests asking an agent what your policy excludes and whether to raise your limits for updates to your home. For turf, make it specific:

1. Which part of my policy, if any, covers my artificial turf: the dwelling, other structures, or trees, shrubs, plants and lawns?
2. Which causes of loss apply? Ask about hail, wind, fire, vandalism and vehicles by name.
3. What limit applies, what shares it, and should I raise it now the turf is in?
4. Which deductible would apply to a turf claim, in dollars?
5. Can I add coverage for it, and what would that exclude?
6. On the coast: does this policy cover wind, and do I need flood coverage?

If the turf sits on a deck, patio or roof attached to the house, ask whether that changes the answer.

## What to keep on file

The sample form's duties after a loss include showing the damaged property, providing the records the insurer asks for, and sending detailed repair estimates with a proof of loss. Keep these with your policy:

- **The written price and the approved layout.** On the free visit we measure the space and check the ground, then you approve a custom layout and get a written price. Our guide to [reading a turf quote](/blog/how-to-read-a-turf-quote/) covers what a complete one lists.
- **The product's [spec sheet](/blog/how-to-read-a-turf-spec-sheet/)**, which names the turf you bought.
- **The invoice and proof of payment.**
- **Dated photos of the finished yard**, plus fresh ones each year before storm season.
- **Your declarations page and your agent's written answer.**

After damage, the NC Department of Insurance's storm advice fits a yard as well as a roof: list the damage and take clear pictures before temporary repairs, keep receipts for what you spend to stop further damage, make no permanent repairs until the insurer has inspected and you have agreed on the cost, and check with the insurer before you dispose of damaged materials. For turf, that means leaving the damaged pieces on site until the adjuster has seen them, unless your insurer says otherwise. If it then needs replacing, see [turf removal and replacement](/services/turf-removal-and-replacement/).

## Checklist before the next storm season

- Write down your dwelling limit, other structures limit and every deductible in dollars.
- Ask your agent, in writing, which coverage applies to your turf and for which causes.
- On the coast, confirm who covers wind and whether you need flood coverage, at least 30 days ahead.
- File the written price, layout, spec sheet, invoice and photos together.
- After a storm, photograph first, protect what you can, keep receipts and leave the damaged turf for the adjuster.
