## Three ways to mark a synthetic field

Lines go on a synthetic field in one of three ways. The Synthetic Turf Council, the industry's trade association, lists them in its field guidelines:

- **Paint,** either temporary or permanent.
- **Tufted or knitted lines,** where colored fiber is built into the turf panels themselves.
- **Inlays,** where strips of turf in a contrasting color are set into the field in place of painted fiber.

The council's key line is short: "Tufted-in or inlaid lines and markings are a permanent part of the surface." Paint is the only method you can take off again.

One caution before you lean on these documents. They are industry guidance, not a legal standard. The council says its guidelines don't replace detailed plans and specifications. It adds that anyone using them still has to meet the laws, regulations and codes that apply.

## Permanent lines: what you gain and what you take on

Tufted and inlaid lines are there from the day the field opens. There's no crew to book before the first game, and nothing to strip off later.

The council asks for a few things so they play like the rest of the field:

- **Matching turf.** The turf and fiber used for the lines should be similar to the rest of the field, installed to the same tolerances.
- **Tight, even joints.** Inlay gaps should be uniform. On tufted systems, the gap between fibers should not exceed the gauge of the tufting.
- **Taped seams.** Seaming tape is commonly used for inlaid lines, with the fabric laid below the backing on both sides of the inlay.

That last point matters for upkeep. Every inlay adds joints. The council's maintenance guidelines tell you to check seams and joints where panels or field markings meet. An open joint can trip a player and should be repaired right away. An opening of 12 inches or less may not mean the seam has failed, so ask your field builder in advance whether self-repair is recommended. Report anything longer to them. More on how joints are built is in our article on [where turf seams go](/blog/where-artificial-turf-seams-go/).

There is one more trade-off, and it's easy to miss. The same guidelines warn that permanent lines and logos "can age differently than the playing field turf." They may harden or shrink at a different rate from the field around them, which will affect Gmax, the surface hardness score. Special grooming or other techniques may be needed.

## Painted lines: flexible, but never finished

Paint lets you change a field's layout. It also puts a job on your calendar for as long as you have the field.

The council's field guidelines say painted lines need maintenance whether the paint is temporary or permanent: "Even permanently painted lines require additional paint on a periodic basis."

Its maintenance guidelines set out how to handle paint on a field:

- **Use the right paint.** Temporary paints can be used if they are formulated for synthetic turf.
- **Paint the fibers, not the infill.** That isn't possible if the infill sits too high, which is one more reason to keep infill at its specified depth.
- **Strip before it builds up.** Remove the paint and start again after a maximum of four applications. Past that, build-up gets hard to remove.
- **Consider a service company.** The council notes that companies with specialized equipment can paint and remove lines, logos, end zones and graphics.

So when you compare methods, count the work as well as the paint: repainting, stripping, and keeping the infill level so the paint lands on the fibers.

## Multi-sport fields: decide which lines are permanent

A field that hosts several sports needs a line plan, not just a line method. The council's guidelines say lines and markings must conform to the guidelines of the relevant association or organization for the intended level of use. Start with each sport's rule book.

Soccer shows how specific those rules get. Law 1 of the [IFAB Laws of the Game](https://www.theifab.com/laws/latest/the-field-of-play/), as published online in September 2026, requires continuous lines "which must not be dangerous." All lines must be the same width, no wider than 12 cm (5 inches). The same law says an artificial surface must be green. Your league may add rules of its own.

With the rules in hand, sort every line into one of two groups:

- **Lines that will not change,** for the sport the field is mainly built for. These are candidates for tufted or inlaid lines.
- **Lines you may add, move or drop,** such as a second sport or a youth layout. These are candidates for paint, because paint can come off.

Multi-sport fields often have inserts too. The council describes covers for goal sleeves and anchors, and turf that converts a baseball infield's clay area. Inserts should use turf similar to the area around them. They should be anchored so play can't move them, and installed to the same tolerances.

## Plan for repairs and testing from day one

Two things are worth settling before the turf is made.

**Spare turf in every line color.** The council says extra turf for future repairs can be supplied, on request and at an agreed price before the field is manufactured. That should include every color used for lines, markings and logos, so repairs use material from the same manufacturing run. It suggests storing the spare turf unrolled outside, so it fades from the sun at the same rate as the field.

**A testing schedule.** The council recommends testing at least at the end of year one and the end of year three, with Gmax included. Because permanent lines can change Gmax as they age, ask the testing firm whether any test points fall on lines or logos. ## Checklist before you sign off the line spec

1. **List every sport and level of play,** and get the line rules for each from its governing body.
2. **Mark each line set on the drawing** as tufted, inlaid or painted.
3. **For inlays,** confirm seaming tape under the backing on both sides and uniform gaps.
4. **For paint,** confirm a product formulated for synthetic turf, and plan a strip-and-repaint after four coats at most.
5. **Order spare turf in every line color** before the field is manufactured.
6. **Book Gmax testing** for the end of year one and year three.
7. **Walk the lines and joints regularly,** and report any open joint longer than 12 inches.
