## Square footage is only the start

Two back yards can measure the same and still be very different jobs. The turf order may match. The digging, hauling, locating, cutting and waiting often don't.

That's why a measurement alone can't set the scope. What adds work is on the ground, and most of it only shows when someone walks the yard. On our free visit we measure the space, check the ground and note each item below. Then we draw a custom layout for you to approve.

## Getting in and out of the yard

Everything that comes out leaves by the same route the base and turf come in. So start with the route:

- **Gate width.** A narrow gate or a side path can decide whether a small machine fits, or the work is done by hand and wheelbarrow.
- **Steps, slopes and distance.** Every step and every extra stretch between the truck and the yard adds trips.
- **Parking.** A long carry from the street moves every load twice.
- **Fences and finished surfaces.** A fence panel that has to come down, or a patio that needs protecting, is part of the job.

## What comes out, and where it can go

A turf job starts by removing what's there: grass and roots, enough soil to make room for the base, and sometimes rock, edging, concrete or old turf. The depth of the dig sets how much of that there is. Atlantic Beach, Florida, for one, requires at least a 4-inch base of permeable, free-draining aggregate. Every inch of base is soil that has to leave first.

It doesn't all go to one place. Disposal sites sort material into streams:

- **Grass and yard waste.** [Horry County's Solid Waste Authority](https://www.solidwasteauthority.org/landfill.html) says "all yard waste is banned" from South Carolina's municipal solid waste landfills under state law. It goes to a separate facility, which turns it into mulch and compost.
- **Concrete, brick and building debris.** The authority's landfill takes shingles, clean wood, concrete and asphalt, brick, mixed construction debris and land clearing debris, and asks that they be kept separate ([Horry County SWA](https://www.solidwasteauthority.org/howdoi.html)).
- **Soil.** Brunswick County, NC lists clean soil with concrete and brick as land clearing and inert debris. Tile, counter tops and painted concrete aren't accepted on the same terms.
- **Construction debris in St. Johns County.** Residents and businesses are required to take it to one of the county's [listed disposal sites](https://www.sjcfl.us/construction-and-demolition-debris-disposal/).

On a [turf replacement](/services/turf-removal-and-replacement/), the old turf is one more load, with whatever infill is still in it.

So the question isn't only how much comes out. It's how many kinds, and how far each has to travel.

## Lines the 811 locate won't mark

Before anyone digs, 811 has to be called. In South Carolina that's at least [three full working days](https://sc811.com/homeowners/faqs-homeowners/) ahead, and no more than 12. In Colorado it's at least 48 hours, not counting weekends, holidays or the day you call ([Colorado 811](https://www.colorado811.org/residential-digging)).

SC811 says the company doing the digging should make the request, and that you should check it has. Colorado 811 adds a warning for landscaping: don't just rely on your landscaper.

The free locate covers public lines only. [SC811](https://sc811.com/homeowners/private-lines/) explains that public lines typically run from the street to your meter, and everything past the meter is private. Its examples:

- sprinkler systems
- gas lines for pools or grills
- electrical wiring to a detached garage or shed
- septic and irrigation lines

Getting those marked is your responsibility. Colorado 811 says that means hiring a private locating company as well as calling 811. If you have site plans for the property, SC811 suggests starting there.

A yard with a sprinkler system to cap or reroute, or a wire running out to a shed, holds more work than a bare lawn the same size.

## What's under the grass

Nobody knows exactly until the sod is up, but the visit narrows it down. The Synthetic Turf Council, the industry's trade association, says in its field guidelines (revised November 2011) that low spots should be filled, and unsuitable material removed and replaced with clean fill or aggregate, before the base goes in. Buried rock, roots, old concrete or soft ground all add to that step.

What's already there can matter too. Atlantic Beach's guidelines ban paver sand, crushed fines, stone screenings and decomposed granite containing fines from the base, because they "restrict infiltration." Where a rule like that applies, an old bed of paver sand comes out rather than staying under the new turf.

## Trees in or near the turf

Trees change both the layout and the dig. Atlantic Beach's guidelines say turf should stay out from under a tree's drip line where possible. Where it has to go there:

- the turf must sit at least 18 to 24 inches from the base of the tree
- the base under the canopy can be no more than 3 inches deep
- that base can only be lightly compacted, and must still let water and air through

That means a separate base section under the canopy, and more cutting around each trunk.

## Shape, roll width and seams

Turf is made in rolls. The Synthetic Turf Council's guidelines say turf is manufactured in panels or rolls "typically a nominal 15 feet wide," and each one is joined to the next with a seam.

So shape matters as much as area:

- **A strip narrower than the roll** can often be covered in one run.
- **A wide rectangle** needs pieces joined side by side.
- **Curves, beds and cutouts** around a patio or tree leave offcuts.

Offcuts can't just be turned to fill a gap. North Carolina's rule for landscape contractors has turf rolled out "with the nap facing a consistent direction" ([21 NCAC 28B .0503](http://reports.oah.state.nc.us/ncac/title%2021%20-%20occupational%20licensing%20boards%20and%20commissions/chapter%2028%20-%20landscape%20contractors/chapter%2028%20rules.pdf)). A piece that fits the space but runs the wrong way can't be used there.

Two yards with the same square footage can need different amounts of turf ordered, and different amounts of seaming.

## A base someone has to inspect

Some places add a step between the base and the turf. Atlantic Beach's guidelines set out:

- **Documents:** the manufacturer's product specifications, permeability testing, installation details or cross-section drawings, and a site plan showing the turf relative to trees and property lines.
- **An inspection:** the aggregate base must be inspected and approved before the turf goes down, and must stay exposed and accessible until approval is granted.
- **Location limits:** no turf in the city right-of-way, and no change to drainage patterns or runoff onto the lots next door.
- **Stormwater areas:** turf in a private retention area can't reduce its storage capacity, and the city may require engineering documentation.

A required inspection means the base is finished, the job pauses, and the turf goes down after approval. That's a second trip, and a schedule set by someone else.

Rules differ by city and county, and they change. Our article on [turf permits](/blog/do-i-need-a-permit-for-artificial-turf/) covers what to ask. Your city or county building or planning office has the final word for your address.

## Before you ask for an estimate

Some of this you can clear up or simplify first:

1. Measure the gate, and walk the route from the street to the yard.
2. Find the sprinkler controller, and any site plan showing where lines run.
3. List what's there now: grass, rock, pavers, concrete or old turf.
4. Note every tree whose canopy reaches the turf area.
5. Ask your county where yard waste, soil and concrete each go.
6. Ask your city or county whether the base has to be inspected.
7. Decide where the turf could stop in a simpler line, clear of trees and beds.
