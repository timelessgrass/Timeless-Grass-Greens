## What a catio floor has to do

A catio is an outdoor space for a cat, enclosed with screen or wire on every side. The environmental guidelines from the [American Association of Feline Practitioners and the International Society of Feline Medicine](https://www.canadianveterinarians.net/media/04gh2nhv/2013-aafp_isfm-environmental-needs-guidelines.pdf) say an enclosure protects a cat from injury and from contact with predators and free-roaming cats.

Turf makes a soft, clean floor for that space. It can't do four other jobs:

- **The toilet.** Cats want loose material they can dig in. Turf is a fixed mat.
- **The scratcher.** A cat that scratches rugs may treat a turf floor the same way.
- **Something to chew.** An indoor cat with no grass may try the closest thing to it.
- **The shade.** A floor in full sun gets hot, and the cat may have nowhere else to go.

## Will your cat use it?

Most of what makes a cat comfortable isn't the floor. The AAFP and ISFM guidelines start with a safe place, often a raised one, and separate spots for food, water, toileting, scratching, play and rest. Shelves, a covered hiding box and a perch with a view matter more than what is underfoot.

The floor does have to smell familiar. Cats use scent to judge whether a place is safe, and new turf smells new. The guidelines suggest rubbing new items with a cloth that carries your cat's scent, or using a synthetic feline pheromone spray, and avoiding scented products in a cat's usual areas.

For the first weeks, put a bed and a scratcher your cat already uses on the turf, and let your cat come and go from the house. The guidelines note that being able to withdraw gives a cat some control over its surroundings.

## Turf is not a litter box

The same guidelines describe a suitable outdoor toilet as a rakeable surface, such as sand or soil the cat can dig in, in a quiet, private spot. Turf is neither loose nor rakeable, so give the catio a real toilet or keep the indoor boxes easy to reach. The [Cornell Feline Health Center](https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/cornell-feline-health-center/health-information/feline-health-topics/feline-behavior-problems-house-soiling) says most cats prefer:

- **Unscented, finer-textured litter** about one to two inches deep.
- **A quiet, private spot** away from where they eat, open to them at any hour.
- **One box per cat, plus one.**

If a box goes in the catio, set it on pavers or tile rather than on the turf, away from the water bowl and the bed. Spilled litter and misses then land on a surface you can wipe and hose.

Planters need a thought too. The [ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/cat-care/common-cat-behavior-issues/litter-box-problems) notes that some cats develop a preference for going on surfaces such as carpet, potting soil or bedding. A pot of loose soil on a catio floor looks a lot like the toilet the guidelines describe.

## If your cat starts going on the turf

Act on the first accident. Cornell says cats that prefer certain surfaces usually stick with that choice.

1. **See your veterinarian.** The ASPCA's first step for a cat going outside the box is to rule out urine marking and medical problems. Cornell adds that cats with medical conditions may not always act sick.
2. **Check for marking.** Spraying usually lands on vertical surfaces, Cornell says, and can start when outside cats are nearby. Through mesh walls your cat sees and smells the neighborhood cats, so the house wall or a post may be the target, not the floor.
3. **Change the spot.** The ASPCA suggests putting a litter box where a cat soils, or moving its food bowl, water, bed or toys there.
4. **Don't punish.** The AAFP and ISFM guidelines say a cat should never be punished for marking or for going outside the box.

## Cat urine needs a faster cleanup

Cat urine is usually stronger than dog urine. The [International Renal Interest Society](https://iris-kidney.com/urine-specific-gravity) says cats typically produce more highly concentrated urine than dogs. For normally hydrated animals it puts urine specific gravity at about 1.035 to 1.060 for cats, against 1.015 to 1.045 for dogs.

The smell has its own chemistry. A [2024 study in the Journal of Veterinary Medical Science](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jvms/advpub/0/advpub_24-0370/_article) traces the catty odor of normal cat urine to compounds made from felinine, an amino acid. The same study found weaker odor in cats with kidney disease, so a change in how your cat's urine smells is worth mentioning to your veterinarian.

On turf:

- **Clean soon.** Cornell says cleaning is most effective soon after soiling, and that odors must be neutralized, not just covered.
- **Use an enzyme cleaner, not ammonia.** The ASPCA recommends an enzymatic cleanser made to neutralize pet odors. Ammonia-based cleaners can draw a cat back to the same spot, it warns, because urine contains ammonia. Cornell adds vinegar.
- **Check the turf maker's instructions** before using any product. Our guide to [cleaning pet turf](/blog/how-to-clean-pet-turf/) covers labels and disinfectants, and [why pet turf keeps smelling](/blog/why-does-artificial-turf-smell-like-dog-pee/) covers infill and drainage.
- **Rinse through, not just over.** [UF/IFAS Extension](https://ask.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/EP612) notes that washing pet urine out of turf often takes quite a bit of water, because it has to pass through the turf, the layer beneath and the weed barrier. On a deck or a slab, that water has to go somewhere.

## Scratching and chewing

Scratching is normal. The [ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/cat-care/common-cat-behavior-issues/destructive-scratching) says cats scratch during play and while stretching, to mark territory and to remove worn outer claws. Some cats prefer horizontal scratchers, and carpeting is among the materials it lists. [Cornell](https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/cornell-feline-health-center/health-information/feline-health-topics/feline-behavior-problems-destructive-behavior) adds that cats are more likely to scratch again where their scent already is.

So give your cat better places to scratch than the floor:

- **A sturdy upright post,** at least as tall as your cat standing on its hind legs with its front legs stretched out, per Cornell. For outdoors, the AAFP and ISFM suggest a wooden post wrapped in sisal rope.
- **A flat scratcher on the turf** if your cat likes rugs, placed where it starts to scratch.
- **Edges and seams held down,** so no loose edge catches a claw. Our article on [dogs that dig](/blog/pet-turf-for-dogs-that-dig/) covers anchoring.

Chewing is the bigger worry. Cornell says cats that chew houseplants are usually indoor cats with little access to grass or other greens, and suggests oat grass, catnip and catmint. Put a few pots in the catio.

Turf blades are plastic strands. The [Merck Veterinary Manual](https://www.merckvetmanual.com/digestive-system/surgical-problems-of-the-gastrointestinal-tract-in-small-animals/gastrointestinal-obstruction-in-small-animals) says cats tend to swallow string-like objects while playing with them, and are more likely to develop an intestinal obstruction from one. Never pull a strand hanging from a cat's mouth: Merck warns it may be anchored, and pulling can cause a perforation. If your cat chews or swallows turf, call your veterinarian.

## Sun on a screened floor

A screen keeps a cat in. It doesn't keep the floor cool. In one study collected by a [2024 review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11272752/), 30 mm synthetic grass averaged about 135°F in sun and 90°F in shade. Our article on [how hot turf gets](/blog/how-hot-does-artificial-turf-get/) has more.

The [Merck Veterinary Manual](https://www.merckvetmanual.com/news/editorial/2025/05/28/17/27/protecting-pets-during-hot-summer-months) warns that heat is especially risky when pets can't find shade or move somewhere cooler, and that cats can burn their paws on hot asphalt or hot sand. A catio is a confined space, so build the way out into it:

- **Solid shade** over part of the floor at the hours your cat is out, from a roof panel, the house or a tree.
- **Fresh water.** The AAFP and ISFM guidelines mention a fountain or a rain-collection dish for outdoors.
- **A way back inside** on hot days, through a cat door or an open door.

If the catio is part of a screened lanai or pool cage, our [screened enclosure article](/blog/artificial-turf-inside-a-screened-pool-enclosure/) covers shade and the pool door.

## What goes under it: ground, slab or deck

What sits under the catio decides where rinse water and rain go.

- **On the ground,** turf goes on a prepared base. See [what goes under artificial turf](/blog/what-goes-under-artificial-turf/).
- **On a concrete patio or lanai slab,** water passes through the turf, then runs across the concrete to a low edge. Our article on [turf over a concrete patio](/blog/artificial-turf-over-a-concrete-patio/) explains the slope to check.
- **On a wood deck,** every rinse wets the boards. The USDA Forest Products Laboratory's [Wood Handbook](https://research.fs.usda.gov/download/treesearch/62262.pdf) says serious decay happens only when wood is wetter than its fiber saturation point, about 30% moisture, and that wood kept air dry won't decay. Where wood can't be kept dry, it advises preservative-treated wood or durable heartwood, naming decking platforms as an example.
- **On a raised deck or an upstairs balcony,** ask a structural engineer about the added weight, wet and dry. Our [balcony and roof article](/blog/artificial-turf-on-a-balcony-or-roof/) explains how that load is counted.

For a deck, ask how the boards will dry after a rinse, whether the gaps between them stay open, and whether the turf can be lifted so you can check the wood.

## Before you order turf for a catio

1. **Pick the litter box spot** on a hard, washable surface.
2. **Plan a tall sisal post** and a flat scratcher.
3. **Add pots of oat grass, catnip or catmint.**
4. **Map the midday shade** and keep a way back indoors.
5. **Ask the turf maker** which urine cleaners suit the turf and infill.
6. **Find where rinse water goes.**
7. **Call your veterinarian** about accidents, chewing or heat stress.
