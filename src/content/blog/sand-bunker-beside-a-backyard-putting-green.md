## Start with the water, not the sand

The [Rules of Golf](https://assets.randa.org/c42c7bf4-dca7-00ea-4f2e-373223f80f76/48712d47-76dc-4fd3-add1-53972c021580/2023%20Rules%20of%20Golf.pdf) define a bunker as "a specially prepared area of sand, which is often a hollow from which turf or soil was removed." The second half of that sentence is what shapes a backyard project. A hollow is a low spot, and a USGA agronomist's advice about grassy hollows applies to any of them: ["Depressions hold water, so be sure to install adequate drainage."](https://archive.lib.msu.edu/tic/groot/article/2010sep34.pdf)

So the first questions are about water, not sand:

- Where does rain already run across the yard?
- Where can the bottom of the hollow drain to?
- Can surface water be kept flowing around the bunker instead of into it?

If those have good answers, a real sand bunker can sit beside a backyard green. If they don't, a turf bunker on a flat or gently shaped zone is the simpler choice.

## How golf courses drain a bunker

A [1999 USGA Green Section Record article](https://gsr.lib.msu.edu/1990s/1999/990514.pdf) describes a Georgia club where putting sand back on the faces after storms took 60 to 100 labor hours, and clay washing in eventually plugged the drain tile. The construction method it describes starts with the outlet, not the sand:

1. Flush dyed water through the outfall pipe to prove it drains.
2. Lay perforated drain pipe (4-inch pipe, in that article) in the compacted subgrade, tied to the outfall.
3. Spread a thin, compacted gravel layer so water reaches the drains without carrying sand.
4. Divert surface water away from the bunker.

A [2025 USGA guide to bunker liners](https://www.usga.org/content/usga/home-page/course-care/green-section-record/63/issue-22/an-updated-guide-for-selecting-bunker-liners.html) backs up that last step: directing surface water away is a low-cost, effective way to limit washouts, and no liner can stop one when a lot of water flows straight in.

Plan the water for the green and the bunker together. Our post on [whether rain drains through artificial turf](/blog/does-rain-drain-through-artificial-turf/) covers the turf side.

## What sand to use, and how deep

A [2020 USGA guide to bunker sand](https://www.usga.org/content/usga/home-page/course-care/green-section-record/58/11/a-guide-to-selecting-the-right-bunker-sand-for-your-course-.html) names grain size and grain shape as the two properties that most influence how a bunker plays. Its starting points come from a 1980s USGA study of 42 course sands:

- About 78 percent of grains between 0.1 and 1.0 mm.
- Less than 15 percent very coarse grains, between 1 and 2 mm.
- Less than 2 percent gravel larger than 2 mm.
- No more than 3 percent silt and clay combined, and less than 5 percent very fine sand.

Shape matters as much. Angular to subangular grains pack firm; rounded grains stay soft and bury the ball. Both can be lab tested, so ask the supplier for results before a load arrives.

Three more points from the guide:

- **Wet climate.** Where heavy rain is frequent, it suggests more coarse grains (10 to 15 percent between 1 and 2 mm) and silt and clay ideally under 2 percent.
- **Wind.** Grains finer than 0.25 mm are more prone to blowing away.
- **Color.** The whiter the sand, the sooner algae shows. Depending on expectations, the guide says a brilliant white sand may only last three to five years.

**Depth.** The guide's minimum is 4 inches on the bunker floor, so a shot never strikes the liner or soil underneath, and 4 to 6 inches has historically worked. Over a liner with the drain pipe underneath it, though, water perches where the sand meets the liner, and the guide reports 5 to 10 inches may be needed before the top inch is dry enough. Laying the drain lines on top of the liner gives water a free path down and cuts the depth needed.

**Faces.** Sand slides off a face that is too steep. The guide's rule of thumb is to avoid bunker faces steeper than 33 degrees.

## Do you need a liner?

The [liner guide](https://www.usga.org/content/usga/home-page/course-care/green-section-record/63/issue-22/an-updated-guide-for-selecting-bunker-liners.html) gives two reasons for one: fewer washouts, and less soil and stone working up into the sand. It also says when you can skip it. Flat or gently sloped bunkers with surface water directed around them don't typically wash out, and bunkers on very sandy soil are unlikely to need a liner.

If you want one, three options suit a small bunker:

- **Fabric.** Low cost and easy to install. Rakes and digging animals can tear it loose, and freeze-and-thaw cycles can lift the staples that hold it down. In the Denver metro, our post on [turf in Colorado winters](/blog/does-artificial-turf-hold-up-in-colorado-winters/) explains frost heave.
- **Synthetic turf liner.** Heavier and more durable than fabric, usually with a piece that covers the edge, and it won't make the sand hold extra water. Its weight makes it harder to install.
- **Porous aggregate** (polymer-bound gravel or porous concrete). Durable and good at holding sand on faces, but it can perch water and keep the sand wet, a particular concern in humid, rainy regions and on frequently irrigated ground. It often needs 6 to 7 inches of sand or more.

A [USGA regional note](https://www.usga.org/content/usga/home-page/course-care/green-section-record/63/issue-06/synthetic-bunker-edges.html) says bunker edges grow unstable as bunkers age, and describes edges built from stacked synthetic turf that hold their shape longer than soil or grass and keep soil from washing into the sand.

## Every shot puts sand on the green

A bunker shot moves sand on purpose, and some of it lands on the green. A golf course superintendent [interviewed by Golf Digest](https://www.australiangolfdigest.com.au/pro-golfers-do-this-every-time-leaving-bunker-slapping-sand-off-ask-a-super) explains why courses care: bunker sand is more angular than the sand spread on greens and has a larger particle size, so sand left on a green before mowing or rolling can damage equipment and shear grass blades.

The USGA guides add that splashed angular sand can grind at turf as feet and equipment pass over it, and that stones splashed out of a bunker can disrupt putting. Those warnings are written for grass, but the habits carry over to a synthetic green:

- Brush or blow splashed sand off after each session instead of walking it in.
- Knock sand off your shoes before you step onto the putting surface.
- Place the bunker so its splash lands where you can sweep it easily, not across your main putting line.

Infill is part of what sets a synthetic green's roll, as our [putting green care guide](/blog/putting-green-care/) explains, so keep extra sand from building up where you didn't put it.

## Leave room to swing

A bunker shot is a full swing taken from inside the hollow, and the clear space runs past the bunker's edge. A [golf net maker's setup guide](https://www.thenetreturn.com/blogs/tips-tricks/net-return-setup-guide-2026-space-ceiling-height-indoor-safety-explained) suggests picturing "a swing bubble of roughly 7 ft around you," mostly clear in the direction the club travels, with about 2 to 4 feet behind you for the backswing. It also says to keep kids and pets behind the hitting line.

Outdoors, look up as well as around. Our post on [room for a golf net or hitting bay](/blog/backyard-golf-net-and-hitting-bay-space/) covers overhead clearance and where a mishit goes. The [USGA agronomist quoted above](https://archive.lib.msu.edu/tic/groot/article/2010sep34.pdf) also notes that seeds, leaves and other debris from nearby trees add to bunker upkeep.

A bunker shot also needs green to land on; see our posts on [how big a backyard putting green should be](/blog/how-big-should-a-backyard-putting-green-be/) and [putting greens versus chipping greens](/blog/putting-green-or-chipping-green/).

## The turf bunker alternative

A turf bunker, sometimes called a faux bunker, is sand-colored synthetic turf laid in a hollow or a flat zone beside the green. Under the Rules of Golf it isn't a bunker, since there is no sand, but it gives you the shape and look of a hazard and a surface to hit from.

[One manufacturer](https://www.synlawngolf.com/golf-turf/bunkers-hazards/) markets its turf bunkers as replicating "the look and challenge of traditional sand or water traps without the maintenance," and contrasts them with sand bunkers that need "constant raking, refilling, and drainage maintenance." That upkeep is real. The [same USGA agronomist](https://archive.lib.msu.edu/tic/groot/article/2010sep34.pdf) writes that bunkers that get a lot of play are raked three to four times a week, sand is added as often as once a year where wind erodes it, heavy rain brings washouts, and bunkers need a full renovation every 10 to 20 years. A backyard bunker gets less play, but the jobs are the same ones.

What to weigh before choosing turf:

- **The shot.** The maker of one white bunker turf says it replicates ["the low-friction feel of sand."](https://www.synlawngolf.com/products/synsport-white) Hit a few shots off a sample to see whether that is close enough for you.
- **Drainage.** A turf bunker set in a hollow is still a low spot and needs the same drainage. The same USGA agronomist warns that a wet hollow can be slippery, so give it a safe way in and out.
- **Splash.** Bunker turf made without sand infill throws no sand onto the green.

## Before the layout is drawn

1. **Where does the water go?** Find the low spots and the outlet first.
2. **Sand or turf?** Real sand shots, or the look and a surface to hit from.
3. **If sand, which sand and how deep?** Get lab results, and match the depth to the liner and drainage.
4. **Is the swing zone clear?** Check around, behind, above and toward the green.
5. **Where will splash land?** Off your main putting line, with a plan to brush it off.
