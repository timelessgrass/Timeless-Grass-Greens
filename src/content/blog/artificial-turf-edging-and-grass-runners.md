## The short version

Grass creeping in from a neighboring lawn is a different problem from weeds sprouting in debris on the turf. That one is covered in [weeds in artificial grass](/blog/weeds-in-artificial-grass/). This one comes sideways, on runners, from a living lawn that keeps growing right up to your edge.

Two things keep it out:

- **A border the runners can't cross**, deep enough that they can't slip under it.
- **A turf edge that stays down**, fastened tight to that border so there's no gap to root in.

Where the turf meets a patio or walk, the same edge has a second job. It shouldn't leave a step to trip on.

## How lawn grass travels

Lawn grasses spread on two kinds of runner. Stolons are stems that run along the ground. Rhizomes are stems that run under it. On St. Augustinegrass, [UF/IFAS explains](https://ask.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/LH010), the nodes along each stolon grow new roots and shoots, and that is how the grass moves across the ground.

Which runners you're dealing with depends on the lawn next door:

- **Bermudagrass** uses both. Clemson's [Home & Garden Information Center](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/bermudagrass/) says it is planted throughout South Carolina, and that its runners, above and below ground, are very difficult to control in flowerbeds, walks and borders. [UF/IFAS](https://ask.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/LH007) says it can rapidly invade flower and landscape beds. [NC State Extension](https://content.ces.ncsu.edu/bermudagrass-lawn-maintenance-calendar) says the same for North Carolina lawns.
- **St. Augustinegrass** is the most commonly used lawn grass in Florida, according to UF/IFAS. It spreads along the ground on stolons.
- **Front Range lawns** use different grasses with the same habit. Colorado State's [Master Gardener notes](https://cmg.extension.colostate.edu/Gardennotes/561.pdf) describe Kentucky bluegrass as sod-forming, with underground rhizomes. They warn that buffalograss's aggressive stolons may invade flower beds and neighboring lawns, and call bermudagrass quite invasive where it is grown in Colorado.

A living lawn fights back against its neighbor. Artificial turf doesn't. [UF/IFAS describes](https://ask.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/AG499) bermudagrass using its stolons to colonize new areas quickly and outcompete other vegetation. At a turf edge there is nothing growing to compete, so a runner stops only where something physical stops it.

## Build a border that goes deeper than the runners

The most specific advice comes from the flowerbed side of the problem. To limit bermudagrass spreading in from other areas, UF/IFAS advises edging materials that extend at least six inches into the soil to separate beds from turf. That is written for living lawns and planting beds. The logic carries straight over to a turf edge: the barrier has to go down, not just along.

What the border can be:

- **A concrete curb or a paver strip**, with the turf edge fastened right up against it.
- **Rigid edging** set to depth along the line where the living lawn stops.
- **A strip of gravel or mulch** between the lawn and the turf. Runners cross open ground readily, which is Clemson's point about walks and borders. But on a strip you can see them and pull them before they reach the turf.

Two things a border can't do:

- **Stop runners already under the turf area.** UF/IFAS says bermudagrass blends so thoroughly into a lawn that physical or mechanical control isn't practical, unless all the vegetation is being removed and the site renovated. For turf, that is the job anyway. The old lawn comes out completely, runners included, before the base goes in. The layers that follow are set out in [what goes under artificial turf](/blog/what-goes-under-artificial-turf/).
- **Be swapped for spraying.** UF/IFAS says no preemergence herbicide is currently labeled to prevent bermudagrass establishing in Florida's warm-season turfgrasses, and that products which injure stolons at a lawn edge are not a dependable way to stop it spreading in. NC State adds that a crisp straight edge is hard to keep with herbicides.

## Hold the turf edge down

An edge that lifts leaves a gap. Soil, runners and toes all find it.

Both written standards we checked require anchoring at the edges:

- **Florida.** [Rule 62-308.100](https://flrules.org/gateway/readFile.asp?sid=0&tid=30839130&type=1&file=62-308.100.doc), in force since 19 May 2026, sets minimum standards for turf on single-family lots of 1 acre or less. It requires turf to be anchored at all edges and seams in a way that at a minimum withstands wind or flooding.
- **Thornton.** The city's [2026 rebate rules](https://www.thorntonwater.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/2026-residential_artificial_turf_info__rules-1.pdf) require anchoring at all edges and seams, using the pins or nails the manufacturer recommends. They also list looseness at edges among the things regular maintenance has to fix.

The edge should also meet the border without a gap. Turf that stops short of the concrete leaves a thin band of soil, and a band of soil is where a runner roots.

## Where turf meets a patio or walk

At paving the question is height. The finished turf surface should meet the paving at close to the same level, so nobody catches a toe on the transition.

The clearest published number is federal. The [US Access Board's guide](https://www.access-board.gov/ada/guides/chapter-3-floor-and-ground-surfaces/) to the ADA Standards says a change in level can be up to 1/4 inch with no treatment. It can be up to 1/2 inch if the edge is beveled no steeper than 1:2. Anything above 1/2 inch has to be treated as a ramp or curb ramp.

Those limits are requirements for accessible routes where the ADA Standards apply. Whether they apply to a particular site is a question for your architect or local building department. On a home lawn they are still a sensible target for any path people walk every day.

Thornton writes the gap into its layout rules. Its 2026 rules keep turf three feet from all property lines, which also keeps it off a neighbor's lawn. Turf can run to the front property line only with a three-foot buffer between it and the street sidewalk. Outside the rear yard or an enclosed side yard, turf stays eight feet from a building's front wall and three feet from other walls and impervious surfaces. Buffers have to be permeable: wood mulch, gravel, or rock no more than two inches across. A Minor Development Permit is required before installing turf as landscaping. Rules like these change, so confirm the current version with the city before you draw a front-yard edge.

## An edge checklist

1. **Name the grass next door.** Bermudagrass, St. Augustinegrass, Kentucky bluegrass and buffalograss all spread sideways.
2. **Mark every edge** where the turf will touch a living lawn, a bed, a patio, a walk or a driveway.
3. **Ask what the border is and how deep it goes.** UF/IFAS's figure for keeping bermudagrass out of beds is at least six inches.
4. **Ask how the edge is anchored**, and whether it's fastened tight against the border.
5. **Clear the old lawn completely** where the turf will go, runners and all.
6. **Check the step at paving.** Aim for 1/4 inch or less where people walk.
7. **In Thornton, check the buffer and permit rules** before you settle a front-yard layout.
8. **Walk the border while the lawn next door is growing**, and pull runners before they root.
