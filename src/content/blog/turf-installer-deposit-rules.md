## What a deposit should pay for

A deposit makes sense when it pays for something specific to your job: the turf, infill and base material ordered for your yard, or a date on the schedule. North Carolina's [Landscape Contractors' Licensing Board](https://nclclb.com/consumer-helpful-tips/) puts it plainly. Paying for services in advance isn't advisable. A down payment may be requested to lock in a quote, a project date or materials, but it should be only a percentage of the project cost, and you should get a detailed receipt saying exactly what it covers.

The [Federal Trade Commission](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-avoid-home-improvement-scam) gives the same advice for any home project: don't pay the full amount up front, and don't make the final payment until the work is done and you're satisfied with it. It adds that some states limit how much a contractor can ask for as a down payment.

None of the state laws we read for our three markets sets a maximum deposit for turf work. What they govern is what happens after you pay: deadlines to start, who can put a lien on your property, and what you can recover if things go wrong. This is general information from the statutes and agency pages linked here, not legal advice.

## Florida: 10% starts a clock, it doesn't set a cap

[Section 489.126](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2026/489.126) applies when a contractor takes more than 10% of the contract price as the first payment for work on residential property. The contractor must then:

- apply for any permits the work needs within 30 days of the payment, and
- start the work within 90 days after the permits are issued,

unless there's just cause or you agreed in writing to a longer period. If that doesn't happen, the statute has you send a written demand by certified mail, return receipt requested, asking the contractor to apply, start or refund. A contractor who still hasn't done one of those within 30 days of receiving it may be inferred to have no just cause. A separate subsection says a contractor holding more of your money than the work done is worth may not go 90 days, or the period your contract sets, without doing any work. Violations are crimes, graded by the amount of money.

Does it reach a turf installer? The section covers anyone doing the work described in [section 489.105(3)](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2026/489.105), with or without a license. That definition is about building, repairing or improving a building or structure, "including related improvements to real estate," where the job is substantially similar to one of its listed trades: general, building and residential contractors, roofing, air conditioning, pools, plumbing, underground utilities, solar and a few others. Landscaping and turf aren't on the list. So the statute doesn't clearly reach a turf-only job, while a job that bundles in work from a listed trade may be a different matter. Either way, the habit it describes is a good one: if the deposit is large, get the order date and the start date in writing.

### The recovery fund covers license holders only

Florida's [Homeowners' Construction Recovery Fund](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2026/489.141) is a last resort, not insurance on a deposit. You generally can claim only after you've won a court judgment, an arbitration award or a licensing board restitution order based on specified violations, used up any bond or insurance, and still couldn't collect. A claim isn't eligible if the contractor didn't hold a valid, current license when you signed.

For contracts signed on or after July 1, 2024, [section 489.143](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2026/489.143) caps payment at $100,000 per claim for Division I contracts (general, building and residential contractors) and $30,000 per claim for Division II, the other trades on the list. Turf isn't one of Florida's contractor categories, so an installer who does turf only may hold no contractor license at all, and then the fund isn't available. [Section 489.1425](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2026/489.1425) requires a written notice about the fund in contracts for work on residential property over $2,500.

### Florida's lien law does cover turf

Florida's lien law counts "grading, seeding, sodding, or planting for landscaping purposes" as improving real property, and lists excavation and landscaping as improvements ([section 713.01](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2026/713.01)). So a turf installer, its subcontractors and the companies that supply the job can have lien rights against your property if they go unpaid.

That's why [section 713.015](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2026/713.015) requires a direct contract over $2,500 for a home of up to four units to carry a lien law notice in capitalized bold type, signed and dated by you. The notice warns that unpaid subcontractors and suppliers may look to your property even if you've paid your contractor in full. It advises writing into the contract that, before any payment, your contractor must give you a written release of lien from anyone who has sent you a "Notice to Owner."

Two more rules matter at payment time. Under [section 713.20](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2026/713.20), a lien right can't be waived in advance, only for work and materials already furnished, and the statute supplies short forms for a waiver on a progress payment and on the final payment. Under [section 713.06](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2026/713.06), when the final payment comes due, the contractor gives you a final payment affidavit listing anyone who served notice and hasn't been paid in full. You keep the final payment until you have that affidavit.

## South Carolina: no deposit limit, but a landscaping lien

We searched South Carolina's [contractor licensing chapter](https://www.scstatehouse.gov/code/t40c011.php) and its [residential builders chapter](https://www.scstatehouse.gov/code/t40c059.php) and found no limit on deposits in either. The residential builders law reaches work on a residential building or structure costing more than $5,000, and the specialty trades it lists, from plumbers and electricians to roofers and masons, don't include landscaping, though the commission can recognize other areas by regulation. Our [installer licensing guide](/guides/turf-installer-licensing-by-state/) covers which board to ask about your full scope.

Liens are a different story. [Section 29-5-26](https://www.scstatehouse.gov/code/t29c005.php) gives a mechanics' lien to anyone who provides a landscape service over $5,000 under a written agreement with you. Landscape service includes grading and other preparation of land, along with drainage, irrigation, paving and walkways. Turf isn't named, but grading and drainage are, and a turf base involves both. Section 29-5-20 in the same chapter gives subcontractors and material suppliers a lien for improvements you authorized, so lien releases are worth asking for here too.

## North Carolina: payment terms in writing

Beyond its down payment advice, North Carolina's landscape board has a rule on paper. Under [21 NCAC 28B .0501](http://reports.oah.state.nc.us/ncac/title%2021%20-%20occupational%20licensing%20boards%20and%20commissions/chapter%2028%20-%20landscape%20contractors/chapter%2028%20rules.pdf), when a contractor holding the board's license provides services worth more than $5,000, they must be described in a written agreement before work starts. The agreement must include the work, the total price, the estimated completion time, the terms of payment and the license number. The rule sets no deposit limit.

The rule binds license holders, and not every installer needs a license. The board's [exemptions page](https://nclclb.com/faq-hold/exemptions/) says its law doesn't apply to landscaping work where all contracts at a job site total less than $30,000 over any 12 months in a row. An installer working under that line isn't bound by the rule, but you can still ask for the same items in writing.

## Colorado and Denver: turf isn't on either list

Colorado's [Division of Professions and Occupations](https://dpo.colorado.gov/About), which handles the state's occupational licenses, lists electrical, plumbing and landscape architects among the occupations it regulates, but not general contractors, landscapers or turf installers. None of the Colorado sources we read sets a deposit limit for turf work.

Denver licenses contractors itself. Its [Community Planning and Development](https://www.denvergov.org/Government/Agencies-Departments-Offices/Agencies-Departments-Offices-Directory/Community-Planning-and-Development/Contractor-Licensing) office issues licenses and certificates for "various types of construction and trade work." Its [specialty certificates](https://www.denvergov.org/Government/Agencies-Departments-Offices/Agencies-Departments-Offices-Directory/Community-Planning-and-Development/Contractor-Licensing/Supervisor-Certificates/Specialty-Class-D-Certificates) include retaining walls, fence structures, and concrete and foundations, but none for ground-level landscaping or turf. If your Denver job adds a wall or a slab, ask who holds the Denver license for that part. Outside Denver, ask the building department for your address.

## If you signed at home and change your mind

The FTC's [Cooling-Off Rule](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/buyers-remorse-ftcs-cooling-rule-may-help) covers many sales made at your home for $25 or more. You can cancel for a full refund until midnight of the third business day after the sale (Saturday counts, Sunday and federal holidays don't), and the seller then has 10 days to refund your money. It doesn't cover sales negotiated at the seller's permanent place of business, or sales made online or by phone. The FTC's home improvement advice adds that a contract signed at home should include a written statement of your right to cancel within three business days.

## Before you pay a deposit

1. Ask what the deposit pays for, and get a receipt that names it.
2. Put the order date and the start date in the contract.
3. Tie each later payment to a finished stage: removal, base, turf down, infill and cleanup.
4. Hold the final payment until the job is finished and you've walked it.
5. Ask for a lien release from the installer with each payment, and from any supplier who sends you a notice.
6. In Florida, get the final payment affidavit before the last check.
7. Write down what happens to the deposit if the job is cancelled or delayed.
8. For any wall, slab, plumbing or electrical work, ask who holds the license for that part.
