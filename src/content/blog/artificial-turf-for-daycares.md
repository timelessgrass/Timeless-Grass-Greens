## Where children can fall, the surface has to give

Falls from equipment are the largest single hazard on playgrounds, according to the [CPSC's Public Playground Safety Handbook](https://www.cpsc.gov/s3fs-public/325.pdf). That's why every state rule below starts with what goes under the equipment. The handbook's baseline:

- **No hard surfaces.** Concrete, asphalt and other hard surfaces should never be directly under playground equipment.
- **No grass or dirt.** They don't count as protective surfacing, because wear and weather reduce how much shock they absorb.
- **Carpet and mats only if tested.** They aren't appropriate unless they're tested to and comply with ASTM F1292, the standard for impact attenuation.
- **Ask for the data.** Anyone installing a manufactured, or "unitary", surface should ask the maker for F1292 test data showing its critical height rating.

The handbook doesn't mention artificial turf by name. Under its approach, turf is judged like any carpet or mat: by its F1292 results. Over a shock pad matched to the equipment's fall height, turf becomes a system that can be tested, and that's the version the state rules make room for. It's also how we plan [commercial turf](/services/commercial-turf/) for play areas.

## Colorado: turf with a pad is on the approved list

Colorado's licensing rules for child care centers, [8 CCR 1402-1, section 2.230](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/colorado/8-CCR-1402-1-2.230), in effect since 15 December 2023, set the outdoor surface rules:

- Climbing equipment 18 inches or higher needs resilient surfacing in its use zone.
- The use zone is 3 feet around toddler climbing equipment and 6 feet around preschool and school-age climbing equipment.
- Approved loose fill includes wood chips, wood mulch, engineered wood fiber, pea gravel, synthetic pea gravel, shredded rubber tires and sand.
- Approved solid, unitary materials are poured-in-place surfacing, approved rubber mats, playground tiles, and "Astroturf with built in resilient pad".
- Any newly installed unitary surface needs written documentation from the manufacturer that it meets current federal safety standards.

The same list appears in the [rules for family child care homes](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/colorado/8-CCR-1402-1-2.337), which also say shade must be available.

Colorado's turf law doesn't stand in the way. The 2026 ban on nonfunctional turf on commercial property doesn't reach a playground, because [HB25-1113](https://content.leg.colorado.gov/sites/default/files/2025a_1113_signed.pdf) defines functional artificial turf to include playgrounds. Our [Colorado turf law guide](/guides/colorado-turf-law/) has the detail.

## South Carolina: cushioning "such as mats, wood chips or sand"

South Carolina's center regulations, [S.C. Code Regs. 114-507](https://www.scstatehouse.gov/coderegs/Chapter%20114.pdf), set the outdoor rules:

- At least 75 square feet of outdoor play space per child.
- Outdoor recreational equipment must meet CPSC standards where they apply.
- Stationary equipment must be firmly anchored and can't sit on concrete or asphalt.
- Cushioning material such as mats, wood chips or sand goes under climbers, slides, swings and large equipment, and extends at least 6 feet beyond the equipment and swings.
- Slides go in a shaded area. Uncoated metal equipment must be shaded or protected from the sun, and teachers check its temperature by touch before children play on it.

Turf isn't named. "Such as" leaves room for other cushioning, but whether turf over a pad counts is DSS licensing's call. Get the answer in writing, and bring the F1292 data when you ask.

## Florida: loose fill or a tested unitary surface

Florida's [Rule 65C-22.001](https://www.flrules.org/gateway/ruleNo.asp?id=65C-22.001), effective 1 September 2026, makes child care programs follow the [Child Care Facility Handbook](https://www.flrules.org/gateway/readRefFile.asp?refId=19085&filename=Child%20Care%20Facility%20Handbook.docx) dated December 2025. For the play yard, the handbook requires:

- At least 45 square feet of usable, safe and sanitary outdoor play area per child aged one and older.
- Shade in the outdoor play area, from trees, buildings or structures.
- A fall/use zone at least 6 feet in every direction from permanent or stationary equipment, with the ground cover kept resilient.
- Loose ground cover at least 6 inches deep. Asphalt, concrete, hard-packed dirt, hay, grass and leaves are unsuitable.
- Unitary surfaces installed and maintained to the manufacturer's instructions, tested to and compliant with ASTM F1292, with the test documentation kept at the facility.

The handbook doesn't name artificial turf either. Turf over a pad would have to qualify as a unitary surface with F1292 data on file, so confirm it with DCF licensing before you build.

One Florida rule doesn't apply to most centers. The state's residential synthetic turf standard, with its natural-infill limit, covers single-family lots of one acre or less, and [DEP's FAQ](https://floridadep.gov/sites/default/files/SyntheticTurfFAQ%20final.pdf) says it doesn't reach commercial property.

## Heat and hygiene

**Heat.** Turf runs hotter than grass in direct sun; our article on [how hot artificial turf gets](/blog/how-hot-does-artificial-turf-get/) has the measurements. The CPSC handbook warns that some dark surfacing in intense sun has caused blistering on bare feet, and suggests shade. Put shade over the busiest play areas first. In Florida it's required anyway.

**Hygiene.** Sand isn't sterile. In a [2020 laboratory study in Sports Health](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7222665/), MRSA stayed recoverable for up to 96 hours on infill and 24 hours on turf fibers. Time to 50% loss of viability ran from 2 hours on EPDM rubber to 27 hours on sand. On a surface children sit and lie on, that calls for a written cleaning routine:

- Remove organic material as soon as possible, as the [Synthetic Turf Council](https://cdn.ymaws.com/staging-stc.site-ym.com/resource/resmgr/guidelines/STC_Guidelines_for_Maintenan.pdf) advises.
- Rinse on a set day. [UF/IFAS](https://ask.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/EP612) notes that many manufacturers recommend weekly wash-downs.
- Use only cleaning products the turf maker approves. The Synthetic Turf Council warns against cleaners with alcohol or acetone solvents.

## A planning checklist for directors

1. **Read your state's rule** and get your licensing contact's answer in writing before anything is ordered.
2. **Mark the use zone** around every piece of equipment.
3. **Get ASTM F1292 test data** for the turf and pad together, at your tallest fall height, and keep it on file.
4. **Plan the shade** over the play areas.
5. **Keep stationary equipment** off concrete and asphalt.
6. **Write the cleaning routine** before opening day.
