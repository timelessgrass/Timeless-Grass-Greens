## The planning decision: distance from the water, not just the address

On a beach or island lot, the layout question isn't only where the turf goes. It's what holds the edge down once it's there, and that answer changes with how close the yard sits to open water.

Florida's rule for synthetic turf treats every lot the same on paper: edges and seams get anchored to withstand wind and flooding, no matter where in the state the lot sits. What the rule doesn't set is the fastener. That's a site decision, and on a lot facing the ocean or the Intracoastal, it's one worth getting right the first time, since a failed edge means turf lifting, seams opening and sand or infill working loose in the next blow.

## What Florida's turf rule requires at the edge

[Rule 62-308.100, F.A.C.](https://www.flrules.org/gateway/readFile.asp?sid=0&tid=30839130&type=1&file=62-308.100.doc), effective statewide since 19 May 2026, covers single-family residential properties of one acre or less. On anchoring, it states plainly that synthetic turf "shall be anchored at all edges and seams that, at a minimum, will withstand the effects of wind or flooding."

The same rule sets three other requirements that touch the same install:

- **Permeable turf on a permeable backing**, over a subgrade prepared for positive drainage.
- **No pooling, and no increase in runoff** onto a neighboring property.
- **Infill that stays put** — the installation has to be designed so infill material doesn't wash off the property.

Anchoring, drainage and infill are one job, not three. An edge that holds against wind but lets water pool behind it, or lets sand wash out from under it in the next heavy rain, hasn't really solved the problem the rule is aimed at.

## Why the fastener itself is a coastal question

Anchoring pattern is only half of it. The hardware has to survive the air it's sitting in.

The [American Galvanizers Association](https://galvanizeit.org/knowledgebase/article/hdg-in-coastal-climates) publishes data on how standard hot-dip galvanized steel performs by distance from open water. Within about a mile of the coast, on surfaces that take ocean wind directly, it reports rusting starting within five to seven years. In a sheltered spot nearby — behind a building, a dune, or a wind break — the same coating has held up fifteen to twenty-five years. Inside 250 feet, the Association flags the exposure as aggressive on its own, driven by direct marine splash or heavy salt spray.

That's a wide range for what looks like the same yard on a map. The Association's own explanation is that the "microenvironment" decides it: which way the prevailing wind runs, how sheltered the lot is, and how often hardware gets wet and dries out again. A backyard three lots off the beach, tucked behind other houses, is not the same exposure as a yard with an open line to the water.

None of this changes what the state rule requires. It changes what "anchored to withstand wind and flooding" should mean in practice on a given lot, and that's a judgment a professional install has to make on-site rather than from an address alone.

## Questions to settle before the layout is final

Bring these to the visit:

1. **How far is the yard from open water**, and does anything — a dune, a building, another row of houses — sit between the perimeter and the wind coming off it?
2. **Which side of the lot takes the wind directly**, versus which side sits in a lee?
3. **Where does storm water already pool or run** on the property today?
4. **Has any metal on the property — railings, fence hardware, a gate — shown rust,** and how old is it? That's a real-world read on the site's own exposure.
5. **Is the lot inside a community with its own layout or fencing rules** that would touch where the turf edge sits?

## Before you approve the layout

1. Confirm the anchoring plan meets Rule 62-308.100's standard for wind and flooding.
2. Ask how the fastener choice accounts for the yard's actual distance from open water.
3. Check that the drainage plan and the anchoring plan work together, not against each other.
4. Walk the perimeter after the free visit and point out anything already showing rust nearby.
5. Confirm the infill plan keeps material on your property in a stiff wind.
