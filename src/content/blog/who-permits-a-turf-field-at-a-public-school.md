## Not the county building department, by default

A homeowner's turf project goes through the city or county — a permit application, plan review, an inspection. A field on public school property runs on a different track in both of our states. The city or county desk that handles a residential yard usually isn't the office reviewing a school district's plans at all.

That surprises a lot of facilities directors and booster clubs the first time they ask. The short version: in Colorado, a state fire agency reviews and inspects school construction unless it has handed that job to a qualified local building department. In Florida, the school board itself is exempt from local permits and carries the code-compliance responsibility on its own shoulders. Neither path starts at the county permit counter.

## Colorado: the Division of Fire Prevention and Control, unless it's delegated

The agency with authority here is the state's Division of Fire Prevention and Control (DFPC), not a city or county building department. C.R.S. 24-33.5-1203 authorizes the Division to "conduct construction plan reviews and inspect public school and local district college buildings and structures and enforce the codes adopted."

That authority isn't fixed to the state office, though. C.R.S. 22-32-124(2)(a)(II) lets the Division "prequalify an appropriate building department to conduct the necessary plan reviews, issue building permits, conduct inspections, issue certificates of occupancy" for a school project. In practice, a lot of districts along the Front Range work through a local building department that has gone through that prequalification, rather than through the Division's own reviewers. Which one is handling a given field depends on whether that arrangement exists for that district, so it's worth asking directly rather than assuming either way.

Two things follow from that split:

- **Who to ask first.** Start with the school district's facilities or capital construction office. They know whether their projects run through DFPC directly or through a prequalified local building department, and they hold the paperwork either way.
- **What the fire and building code review does and doesn't cover.** It's a construction code review — structural, fire and life-safety requirements for the buildings and structures. It is not a zoning or land-use approval, which is a separate step below.

## Colorado also requires a site development plan to the local planning commission

Fire and building code review is one track. Land use is another, and it still runs through the local government. C.R.S. 22-32-124(1)(a) requires the board of education to "submit a site development plan for review and comment to the planning commission or governing body" before construction begins, and to "consult with and advise in writing" that body.

Notice the language: review and comment, not approval. A planning commission weighing in on a school site plan doesn't have the same veto a homeowner's zoning application might face. But it's a real, required step, and it's where questions about setbacks, parking, lighting for a field, or neighboring land use tend to surface. If a booster club or facilities director is trying to map out a timeline for a new or renovated field, this is the meeting to find on the district's calendar, separate from any fire-code plan review.

All buildings and structures still have to be "constructed in conformity with the building and fire codes adopted by the director of the division of fire prevention and control," per the same statute — so the substantive standards a project is built to come from the state, even when a local department is the one doing the reviewing.

## Florida: the school board carries the compliance, not the county

Florida takes a different approach. Under [Fla. Stat. 1013.371](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2025/1013.371), school board "plants" — its facilities — "are exempt from all other state building codes; county, municipal, or other local amendments to the Florida Building Code and local amendments to the Florida Fire Prevention Code; building permits, and assessments of fees for building permits."

That exemption doesn't mean nobody is checking the work. The same statute puts the responsibility squarely on the board: "It is the responsibility of each board to ensure that all plans and educational and ancillary plants meet the standards of the Florida Building Code and the Florida Fire Prevention Code and to provide for the enforcement of these codes in the areas of its jurisdiction."

The statute spells out how a board does that. It may:

- Employ its own chief building official and other certified inspectors, plus a fire official, or
- Use local building department inspectors who are certified to enforce the Florida Building Code, or
- Use local county, municipal, or independent special fire control district inspectors certified by the State Fire Marshal.

So a county or city inspector can end up on a school field project in Florida — but as someone the board selected to do the work, not as the agency with jurisdiction over it. The permit and the enforcement authority sit with the board either way.

## What this means for a booster club or facilities director

If a booster club, parent group, or outside vendor is pushing a turf field project forward on school property, the practical difference is where the paperwork starts:

- **It's not the city or county permit counter**, in either state, for the school-owned facility itself.
- **In Colorado**, ask the district which authority — the Division or a prequalified local building department — is reviewing the current project, and confirm the site development plan has gone to the local planning commission.
- **In Florida**, ask the district's facilities office who its designated inspectors are for the project: its own certified staff, or local building department or fire district inspectors it has selected.
- **A booster club paying for or organizing the work doesn't change who holds the permitting authority.** That stays with the district or board even when outside money is funding the field.

## A short checklist before the project timeline is set

1. Confirm which office is reviewing the plans in Colorado: DFPC directly, or a prequalified local building department.
2. Confirm the site development plan has gone to the local planning commission for review and comment.
3. In Florida, confirm who the board has designated to enforce the Building Code and Fire Prevention Code on this project.
4. Get the answer in writing from the district's facilities or capital construction office before locking a start date.
5. Keep the school permitting track separate from the vendor's own site work — they run on different timelines and different rules.
