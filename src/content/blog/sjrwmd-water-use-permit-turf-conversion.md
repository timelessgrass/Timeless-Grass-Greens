## The decision first

Converting a golf course, entry median or shared lawn to turf doesn't require a new St. Johns River Water Management District permit application on its own, and it doesn't automatically cut the community's permitted allocation either. What it does require is documentation, made at the time of the conversion, that ties the resulting drop in water use to a conservation measure rather than to something the district might read as declining need.

That distinction matters because the district treats the two causes differently. Get the paperwork right when the turf goes in, and the community's existing allocation is protected through the rest of the permit term. Get it wrong, or skip it, and a lower-than-permitted use number sitting in the file for years can become an opening for the district to reduce the allocation the next time it looks.

## What the permit rule requires

Golf courses, water-based recreational areas and athletic fields fall under the "landscape/recreation/aesthetic" use type in the district's [Applicant's Handbook: Consumptive Uses of Water](https://flrules.org/gateway/readRefFile.asp?refId=4442&filename=SJRWMD+CUPcon+AH+4-3-14+Final+Clean+w+NOC+made.doc), and a permit for that use type already carries its own water conservation plan requirement under the handbook's section 2.2.6. If your community's common-area irrigation is permitted under this category, that plan is the document a turf conversion should be added to — not a new filing, but an update to what's already on record.

The protection for that documented conservation shows up in section 1.5.4, under the handbook's compliance-report provisions: when actual water use comes in below the permitted amount because of documented water conservation measures, the district will not modify the allocation for that reason during the permit term. A turf conversion that removes irrigated turfgrass from a fairway, entry corridor or common lawn is a conservation measure in exactly that sense, provided it's written down as one — the acreage converted, the date, and the reduction in irrigated area it produced.

Section 1.5.4 draws a second line that matters just as much. When a drop in use comes from something else — economic conditions, population changes, fewer rounds played, an amenity used less than it once was — a reduction to the permitted allocation happens only when the district finds no reasonable likelihood the water will be needed again during the permit term. That's a different, and less favorable, standard than the conservation protection. A board that lets a turf conversion's water savings sit in the file without a conservation designation risks having that drop read under the second standard instead of the first.

Permits issued for 20 years or longer carry a compliance report requirement of their own: under section 1.5.4, one is due once every ten years. That report is where the record of a documented conservation measure, dated to when the conversion happened, belongs. If your permit is due for a report in the next few years, get the turf conversion's paperwork in order well before then.

Rule [40C-2.101](https://flrules.org/gateway/ruleNo.asp?id=40C-2.101) is the district's rule that adopts the Applicant's Handbook by reference, and it was amended 11 December 2025 to incorporate a newer edition. The section numbers above come from the 2014 edition cited in many existing permit files; ask the district which edition of the handbook governs your community's specific permit before you rely on any one section for a document going to your board or the district. Many changes to a valid permit, including this kind of update, can be requested by letter rather than a full new application, under the letter-modification process described in rule [40C-2.331](https://flrules.org/gateway/ruleNo.asp?id=40C-2.331) and the handbook itself.

## What a professional install plans for

A turf conversion on permitted common ground is a site-prep and layout project before it's anything else, and the permit paperwork runs on its own track alongside it. We measure the areas coming out of irrigation at a free visit, check the ground, and draw a [custom layout](/services/commercial-turf/) your board or property manager approves before work starts. From there it's site prep and a base built for the soil, turf laid, seamed and edged, infill and finishing, and a walkthrough of the upkeep it needs.

We don't file anything with SJRWMD, and we don't fill out the conservation plan update or the compliance report. That paperwork is between the community, its counsel or engineer, and the district — the same way a [CDD or HOA's architectural review](/blog/cdd-or-hoa-who-approves-turf/) is a separate track from what we handle on the ground. What we can hand back is exact: the acreage converted, dated by section of the property, so whoever prepares the district paperwork has real numbers rather than an estimate.

## Questions to settle before the project is final

Before a layout goes final on a permitted common area, a board, property manager or course superintendent should have answers to:

1. **What use type and category is the existing consumptive use permit filed under**, and does it already carry a water conservation plan?
2. **How many irrigated acres will the turf conversion remove**, and can that figure be documented by section or hole so it matches district recordkeeping?
3. **When is the permit's next compliance report due**, if it runs 20 years or longer, and does the conversion's timing need to land before that filing?
4. **Who is preparing the conservation plan update or letter modification** — the community's engineer, its water management consultant, or counsel — and when do they need the converted-acreage numbers from us?
5. **If the community [backs onto a golf course](/blog/backing-onto-a-golf-course-easements-and-turf/) or shares a permit across multiple parcels**, does the conversion affect only your section, or does it change reporting for the whole permitted area?

## Before you call the district

Bring these to whoever handles your permit, then bring your board's approved layout to us:

- Confirm which edition of the Applicant's Handbook governs your permit, and pull section 1.5.4 from it.
- Get the converted acreage in writing, dated, before the conversion starts.
- Ask whether the update goes in as a documented conservation measure or waits for the next compliance report.
- Check whether a letter modification is enough, or whether the district wants something more.
