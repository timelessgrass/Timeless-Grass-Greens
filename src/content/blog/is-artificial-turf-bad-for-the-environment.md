## What turf saves

The case for turf starts with water. A lawn that's gone doesn't need sprinklers. [Resource Central](https://resourcecentral.org/lawn/), which runs lawn replacement for more than 30 Front Range water providers, counts about 10 gallons saved for every square foot of lawn converted, every year.

The rest of a lawn's inputs go too. [New Jersey's Department of Environmental Protection](https://dep.nj.gov/wp-content/uploads/dsr/synthetic-turf-report-2025.pdf) lists what turf skips: mowing, seeding, watering, pesticides and fertilizers. [UF/IFAS Extension](https://ask.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/EP612) confirms that synthetic turf needs no fertilizer.

Two limits on that:

- **It isn't zero water.** UF/IFAS notes that many manufacturers recommend a weekly wash-down to clear dust and pet waste, and that people spray turf to cool it on hot days.
- **It isn't what Colorado calls water-wise.** The state's turf replacement program excluded artificial turf by name, and most Denver-metro rebates exclude it too. Our [Colorado turf law guide](/guides/colorado-turf-law/) sets out the statute.

## Microplastics

This is the cost that gets the most attention, and the evidence is solid. New Jersey DEP's 2025 report found that sunlight, wear and weather break turf fibers and infill down into microplastics, and that fibers shed continually during use. Crumb rubber infill doesn't even need to break down: the granules are already small enough to count as microplastics.

The particles leave in rain runoff, on the wind, on shoes and clothes, and during maintenance. For sports fields, NJ DEP cites a European Chemicals Agency estimate of 16,000 tons of infill lost each year across Europe, about 900 kilograms per field. The European Union has since acted: [granular infill for synthetic sports surfaces can't be sold there from 17 October 2031](https://trade.ec.europa.eu/access-to-markets/en/news/restriction-microplastics-eu-17-october-2023).

A back yard isn't a sports field, and those figures are for fields. Two things lower the load at home:

- **Infill choice.** Florida already limits infill on single-family lots to clean silica sand, rock, shell or other natural material, with rubber allowed only under playground equipment. Our [Florida infill guide](/guides/florida-turf-infill-rules/) covers the rule.
- **Maintenance.** NJ DEP puts most off-site migration down to maintenance practice: leaf blowing instead of raking, poor snow clearing and piling, and nothing in place to catch loose material. Rake or blow gently, and keep infill out of the gutter.

## Heat and runoff

Turf runs hotter than grass in direct sun. UF/IFAS reports natural lawns measured as much as 100°F cooler than synthetic surfaces. Our piece on [how hot artificial turf gets](/blog/how-hot-does-artificial-turf-get/) covers what shade and water do about it.

Runoff is the cost fewer people know about. UF/IFAS cites research finding more runoff and less water retention on synthetic lawns than on living ones, and zinc in synthetic turf runoff at levels that pose a potential risk to surface waters. Its [extension blog](https://blogs.ifas.ufl.edu/global/2025/07/15/considering-synthetic-turf-what-homeowners-should-know-before-installation/) puts part of that down to the build: turf laid over compacted soil slows infiltration and speeds runoff.

The build can be done better. [Florida's standard](https://www.flrules.org/gateway/readFile.asp?sid=0&tid=30839130&type=1&file=62-308.100.doc) requires the turf, backing and subgrade to be permeable, and bars compacting the soil beneath to the point that percolation suffers. Even then, the ground has the last word. As NJ DEP puts it, a turf product that drains 10 inches an hour doesn't make the soil under it drain that fast.

## Wildlife and soil

There's no case to make here. UF/IFAS says synthetic turf offers nothing that attracts or supports wildlife. Its blog adds that turf doesn't support pollinators or beneficial insects, doesn't improve soil health and doesn't help store carbon. It's also why UF/IFAS says turf can't count as a plant choice under Florida-Friendly Landscaping.

## End of life

Turf wears out. NJ DEP expects sports fields to last about 10 to 12 years with proper maintenance, and UF/IFAS gives 10 to 20 years for home lawns. Then it has to go somewhere.

NJ DEP found that landfill or incineration is standard practice, whatever is claimed about recycling, and that very few reuse or recycling options exist yet. Up to 90% of the sand and rubber infill can be vacuumed out before removal. But there's little evidence it can be separated cleanly enough to reuse, and weathered plastic tends to be downcycled into something of lower quality. Florida's standard assumes the landfill: it requires turf on covered lots to be disposable at a permitted landfill.

## Weighing it up

NJ DEP's summary of life cycle studies is the fairest short answer we found. Per hour of play on a busy field, synthetic turf generally comes out ahead of natural grass. But those studies leave out microplastics, and making turf takes large amounts of water, energy and fossil fuels.

A back yard isn't a stadium. Turf earns its place where you'd otherwise pour water and effort into grass that struggles: a dog run that's mud half the year, a shaded strip that never took, a putting green. It makes less sense as a blanket over every square foot. Plants do things turf can't.

## If you go ahead, go lighter

1. **Turf only where you'll use it.** Plant the rest.
2. **Natural infill** where you can. On Florida single-family lots it's the rule.
3. **A permeable base**, without over-compacting the soil beneath it.
4. **Rake or blow gently**, and keep infill out of the gutter and the storm drain.
5. **Ask about the end.** How would the old turf and infill come out, and where would they go?
